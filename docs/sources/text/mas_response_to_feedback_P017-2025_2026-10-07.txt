Response to Feedback Received on Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence Risk Management 
 
1. Preface 
1.1. On 13 November 2025, MAS issued a consultation paper on the proposed Guidelines on Artificial 
Intelligence (AI) Risk Management (the “Guidelines”) to set out its supervisory expectations relating to 
AI risk management in the financial sector. The Guidelines, which apply to all financial institutions (FIs), 
focus on oversight of AI risk management, key AI risk management systems, policies and procedures, 
key AI life cycle controls, as well as capabilities and capacity needed for the use of AI. 
1.2. The consultation period closed on 31 January 2026, and MAS would like to thank all respondents for 
their contributions. The list of respondents is in Annex A. 
1.3. MAS has carefully considered the feedback received and appreciates the views shared. Where 
appropriate, the feedback has been reflected in the final Guidelines, having regard to the Guidelines’ 
overall objectives. The final Guidelines can be accessed via the following link: Guidelines on Artificial 
Intelligence Risk Management.  
1.4. Comments that are of wider interest, together with MAS’ responses are set out below.

2. Applicability of the Guidelines 
Scope of Guidelines 
2.1. MAS had proposed to apply the Guidelines to all FIs. FIs in Singapore that are branches or subsidiaries 
with parent entities in other jurisdictions may leverage the AI risk management frameworks of their 
parent entities, as long as such frameworks meet the expectations set out in the Guidelines. 
2.2. There was broad support for the proposal. Two respondents sought clarification on whether the 
Guidelines would apply to FIs which are incorporated outside of Singapore and foreign subsidiaries of 
FIs incorporated in Singapore.  
MAS’ Response 
2.3. The expectations on AI risk management set out in the Guidelines are relevant to all FIs deploying AI, 
and apply regardless of where an FI is incorporated. The Guidelines apply on a group basis for an FI with 
a branch or subsidiary under it, and which is: i) subject to consolidated supervision by MAS1; or ii) an 
owner of critical information infrastructure2. 
Scope of AI 
2.4. MAS proposed the scope of AI to cover AI use cases, systems and models. This extends to third-party 
AI, which encompasses all providers of third-party AI services, covering third-party systems, models, as 
well as data used for AI. Existing third-party services where AI has been introduced would also fall within 
scope. 
2.5. Some respondents sought clarification on whether certain use cases, systems and models fall within 
the proposed scope of AI. In particular, respondents asked whether hybrid systems with both rule-
based and AI components, embedded AI incorporated within, or used in the delivery of, third-party 
services not explicitly marketed or sold as AI, conventional risk models (e.g. logistic regression), and AI 
in pilot or proof-of-concept stage, are within scope, and whether the scope of AI is limited to only use

environments. Different AI vary in their levels of autonomy and adaptiveness after deployment. 
Calculators or tools whose outputs are solely based on predefined programming logic or rules would 
not be regarded as AI for the purpose of the Guidelines.

2.10. With regard to third-party AI, risks may arise not only from AI services procured from third parties, but

adopt a risk-proportionate approach towards the identification of third-party AI. This is further 
discussed under Section 6 of this response paper. 
3. Proportionality 
3.1. MAS had proposed that an FI should implement the Guidelines in a risk-based and proportionate 
manner. All FIs should establish basic AI governance policies and procedures for the use of AI 
commensurate with its level of AI adoption. The full set of expectations in the Guidelines would apply 
only if the FI used AI as an “integrated” part of its business processes. 
3.2. In the consultation paper, MAS proposed that AI would be regarded as being used as an integrated part 
of an FI’s business processes where either: 
(a) the lack of access to AI services or tools would disrupt workflows that the FI is materially 
dependent on for its business activities; or 
(b) the AI is integrated with systems that the FI is materially dependent on for its business activities.  
Proportionate application of AIRG 
3.3. Respondents were broadly supportive of the proposal to apply the Guidelines in a proportionate 
manner. However, a few respondents disagreed with using operational dependence as the primary 
criterion to determine whether an FI is subject to the full set of expectations in the Guidelines, and

productivity tools (e.g. Microsoft Copilot). Respondents also flagged practical challenges in assessing 
the extent to which AI has been integrated into workflows or systems, and suggested that the scoping 
should instead focus on risk materiality and impact, for example whether the failure or poor 
performance of AI tools or services would materially affect the FI, its customers, or its ability to meet 
regulatory obligations. 
3.4. A few respondents disagreed with the classification of an internal IT helpdesk chatbot as an AI use case 
that is an integrated part of an FI’s business process in the Annex of the Guidelines. Respondents also 
requested additional examples to guide FIs in their assessment of whether their AI use is considered as 
an integrated part of FI’s business process to ensure consistent application of the AIRG. 
3.5. A few respondents requested flexibility for FIs to scale the extent of oversight and AI risk management 
systems, policies and procedures proportionately based on the risk materiality of each individual use 
case, rather than applying all controls uniformly across all integrated AI use cases. They highlighted that 
not all AI use cases should attract the same level of controls, and more extensive controls should be 
reserved for AI use cases assessed to be of high risk materiality. Respondents also suggested the degree 
of reliance on AI should be a factor in determining the extent of controls to be applied for an AI use 
case. 
MAS’ Response 
3.6. MAS agrees that the workflow-based criterion to determine whether an FI is subject to the full set of 
expectations in the AIRG may unintentionally capture FIs whose AI use is limited to only assistive AI 
tools where poor performance or unavailability of such tools is unlikely to have a material adverse 
impact on the FI, its customers or other stakeholders. 
3.7. Under the revised approach, an FI may apply basic AI governance policies and procedures to oversee 
its AI use, if the poor performance or unavailability of the AI services or tools used by the FI is unlikely 
to have a material adverse impact on the FI, its customers or other stakeholders. Otherwise, the FI 
should implement the full set of expectations set out in Sections 3 to 6 of the Guidelines. These changes 
are reflected within Section 2 of the Guidelines. 
3.8. MAS has also incorporated examples of assistive AI use cases where an FI may implement basic AI 
governance policies and procedures within paragraph 2.4. of the Guidelines. These examples include 
using AI to assist in drafting or proofreading emails, summarising documents or meeting notes, or 
generating charts and visualisations for internal reference. Humans involved in these AI use cases would

3.9. MAS further clarifies that Sections 3 to 6 of the Guidelines should be implemented in a risk 
proportionate manner. As set out in paragraph 2.2. of the Guidelines, an FI should establish a 
framework for oversight, including identification, inventorisation and risk materiality assessment of AI 
use cases. The extent of oversight and the design of AI risk management systems, policies and 
procedures may be proportionate to the size and nature of the FI’s activities, its use of AI, and risk 
profile of its AI use cases. 
3.10. At the use case level, an FI should conduct a structured risk assessment across multiple dimensions, 
including the complexity of the AI technology and the degree of reliance on AI for the decision or 
output. Based on this assessment, the FI has the flexibility to calibrate the extent of lifecycle controls 
to be applied. For example, controls relating to transparency, explainability, and fairness should be 
applied with greater rigour for use cases of higher risk materiality, such as credit scoring, insurance 
underwriting, and the provision of financial advisory or fund management services, where failure, 
malfunction, or poor performance of AI could have potentially significant consequences on the FI’s 
customers. Further details on the risk materiality assessment are set out in paragraph 4.10. to 4.13. of 
the Guidelines.

commensurate with the level of AI adoption of such FIs.

(c) Frequency of review: MAS has amended paragraph 2.5.(f) of the Guidelines to provide an FI with

4. Responsibilities of the Board and Senior 
Management 
4.1. MAS sought comments on the proposed responsibilities of the FI’s board and senior management in 
overseeing AI risk management. Such responsibilities included maintaining effective oversight of risks 
from AI use, fostering the appropriate risk culture for the use of AI, ensuring that risk management 
frameworks adequately identify, assess and address risks posed by AI, and ensuring that the FI’s use of 
AI would not conflict with its ability to meet other supervisory expectations. 
Discharge of responsibilities by other committees or 
structures 
4.2. Respondents were broadly supportive of the proposed responsibilities of the FI’s board and senior 
management. A few respondents requested guidance on the extent to which an FI which is part of a 
global group could rely on global or regional-level governance structures to fulfil the proposed 
responsibilities, to avoid duplication of governance structures. A few respondents sought clarity 
whether such responsibilities could be discharged via existing Board committees or delegated to 
existing individuals or committees which had the relevant expertise for AI risk management. 
MAS’ Response 
4.3. An FI that is part of a global group may discharge board responsibilities either through a board-level 
committee delegated by the board of directors, or through a governing body or committee beyond 
local management (such as a regional or global committee) that exercises oversight of the risks from AI 
use relevant to the FI in Singapore. For other FIs, board responsibilities may be discharged via a board-
level committee. A board may delegate certain responsibilities to senior management committees, but 
it retains overall accountability. 
4.4. As stated in paragraph 1.5. of the Guidelines, an FI which is part of a global group may leverage the AI 
risk management frameworks of their parent entity to meet the expectations in the Guidelines.

Notwithstanding this, local senior management should still have adequate visibility into how the risks 
from AI use relevant to Singapore operations are being managed and remains accountable for ensuring 
that group frameworks adequately address local considerations and regulatory requirements. An FI 
should be able to demonstrate how such oversight is discharged, including where reliance is placed on

Responsibilities of board versus senior management 
4.5. A few respondents requested greater delineation of the expected responsibilities of the board versus 
that of senior management. It was highlighted that certain aspects of the Guidelines referred to the 
board and senior management collectively without distinction. 
4.6. A few respondents also sought clarification on the expectations for the board (or a committee 
delegated by it) to approve key frameworks, structures, policies and procedures. In particular, one 
respondent suggested allowing the board to delegate approval of technical procedures to senior 
management or a specialised committee. 
MAS’ Response 
4.7. MAS has further streamlined the Guidelines to enhance the clarity of the respective responsibilities of 
the board versus that of senior management. The board will be responsible for areas such as approving 
the overall governance approach and key frameworks, and ensuring that risks from AI use are 
addressed in the FI’s risk appetite framework. Senior management will be responsible for effective 
implementation of the AI risk management framework, instituting appropriate controls to manage risks 
from AI use, and establishing clear roles and responsibilities for AI risk management across the FI. 
Collectively, the FI’s board and senior management should maintain effective oversight of risks from AI 
use. 
4.8. MAS only expects the board (or a committee delegated by it) to approve key frameworks, structures, 
policies and procedures which set out the overall governance approach to AI risk management. Such 
documents could cover areas such as the definition and scope of AI, governance and oversight 
structures for AI risk management, and setting the overarching tone and guiding principles for use of 
AI across the FI. Operational or technical frameworks, structures, policies and procedures, which may 
cover areas such as the FI’s approach to risk materiality assessment, or the application of lifecycle 
controls, may be approved by senior management or relevant committees as appropriate.

Further clarifications on board responsibilities 
4.9. A few respondents sought greater clarity on specific board responsibilities, including: 
(a) Technical competence: The extent to which the board should possess technical competence, 
receive specialised training, or rely on experts within or outside the FI, to ensure adequate 
understanding of AI. 
(b) Risk appetite: Guidance on incorporating risks from AI use within the FI’s risk appetite framework, 
including clarification on what would be considered acceptable risk appetite statements or 
measures, and the specific expectations on the board given that this technical process of defining 
specific risk appetite statements or measures was usually handled by the FI’s functional risk 
management forums. 
(c) Other responsibilities: Whether certain other responsibilities not mentioned in the Guidelines 
should be explicitly assigned to the board, such as setting the strategic direction of AI use or 
approving use cases with high risk materiality. 
MAS’ Response 
4.10. MAS’ responses to the respective items are as follows: 
(a) Technical competence: The board is not expected to possess hands-on technical expertise to 
challenge AI use at a granular level. However, it should maintain a sufficient understanding of risks 
arising from the FI’s adoption of AI to provide effective oversight. This is consistent with how 
boards oversee other complex or technical risks, such as technology risk.  
The board should be able to provide effective challenge on matters such as senior management’s 
assessments of material risks from AI use, the adequacy of the overall control environment, and 
whether the FI’s use of AI is consistent with the FI’s risk appetite and strategic objectives. To meet 
this expectation, the board may determine the most appropriate approach, which may include 
having relevant expertise on the board itself and/or supplementing its collective expertise through 
other means, such as regular training or independent expert input. 
While the board may receive advice from experts within or outside the FI, such advice is not a 
substitute for the board’s own oversight responsibilities. The board remains accountable for 
exercising its own judgement in discharging its oversight role. 
(b) Risk appetite: MAS’ intent is for the risk appetite framework to address AI-specific risks. An FI has 
flexibility to determine how best to incorporate such risks into its existing risk appetite framework.

The Guidelines have been updated to provide illustrative examples of qualitative statements or

Three lines of defence and role of Audit 
4.11. A few respondents sought guidance on the application of the ‘three lines of defence’ structure to the 
assignment of roles and responsibilities in AI risk management, including examples of roles and 
responsibilities envisaged from each line of defence. Two respondents suggested more explicitly setting

responsibilities for AI risk management. FIs should adopt governance structures suited to their business 
context, provided these ensure clear accountabilities and appropriate segregation of duties. The 
Guidelines have been amended under footnote 19 to give some illustrative examples of the potential 
roles and functions of each line of defence, including the role of internal audit as the third line of 
defence under a ‘three lines of defence’ structure.

dedicated cross-functional committee to ensure adequate AI oversight and proactively address 
potential gaps in risk management coverage.

Need for a dedicated cross-functional committee 
5.2. While respondents broadly agreed on the need for robust cross-functional oversight of AI, views were 
mixed on whether a cross-functional committee was necessary to ensure such oversight. In particular, 
some respondents expressed concerns with the expectation for the committee to be dedicated (i.e. 
with sole focus on AI oversight), as this may be duplicative with existing governance structures, some 
of which may already involve cross-functional committees. It was suggested to give the FI flexibility to 
determine appropriate structures for ensuring adequate AI oversight, whether via existing cross-
functional committees, or expanding the terms of reference of existing specialised committees (e.g. 
technology risk, model risk) to cover risks from AI use. 
MAS’ Response 
5.3. MAS has amended paragraph 3.3. of the Guidelines to recognise that FIs have the flexibility to adopt 
varying approaches to AI risk management. Such approaches include creating new centralised functions 
dedicated to risks from AI use, or managing the incremental risks from AI use via existing risk 
management functions. Regardless of the operating model, the FI should still ensure that there is a 
consolidated view of risks from AI use across the organisation and that such risks are managed in a 
consistent and coordinated manner across all relevant functions, whether via a dedicated cross-
functional committee, or other appropriate structures. An FI should be able to demonstrate how its 
chosen approach achieves this objective. 
Expectations on the cross-functional committee 
5.4. Some respondents proposed that the Guidelines recognise that the dedicated cross-functional 
committee could be established at global or regional level, to ensure consistency in AI risk management 
across the entire organisation, and to avoid creating duplicative structures at the Singapore level. 
5.5. A few respondents requested greater clarity, as well as provided suggestions, on the expected 
responsibilities of the dedicated cross-functional committee. Suggestions fell largely into two groups: 
(a) Strategic responsibilities, such as oversight, ethical alignment, risk appetite, and approval of 
policies and procedures. 
(b) Operational responsibilities, such as approving certain AI use cases, monitoring key risk indicators, 
reviewing AI incidents, and providing relevant reporting to the Board and senior management.

MAS’ Response 
5.6. MAS agrees that for an FI which is part of a global group, such dedicated cross-functional committees 
could be set up at the global or regional level. However, the FI should be able to demonstrate how the 
dedicated cross-functional committee ensures consistent and coordinated management of risks from

6. AI Identification & Inventory  
6.1. MAS had proposed for an FI to establish systems, policies and procedures to ensure the consistent 
identification of AI use across all relevant business and functional areas. Clear definitions, criteria and 
processes, supported by robust systems, should be implemented to facilitate this identification process. 
6.2. MAS also proposed for an FI to establish and maintain an accurate and up-to-date inventory of AI use 
cases, systems or models across the FI. While an FI could enhance existing inventories to include AI use 
cases, systems or models or establish a dedicated inventory for AI use cases, the FI should establish 
clear linkages between the AI inventory and other relevant inventories in the FI. 
Scope of AI to be identified & incorporated into inventory 
6.3. While respondents generally agreed with the principle of comprehensively identifying AI use, a few 
respondents expressed reservations over an FI’s ability to practically implement processes that achieve 
coverage of all AI use. In particular, challenges with identifying two types of AI were highlighted: 
(a) Embedded AI: AI incorporated within, or used in the delivery of, third-party services to the FI, 
where the services are not explicitly marketed or sold as AI. Challenges to AI identification could 
arise where providers incorporate or use AI within third-party services without disclosure or prior 
notification to the FI. 


===== PAGE 16 =====
7 OCTOBER 2026 | 16

(b) Shadow AI: Use of public AI tools by personnel in the FI. Challenges to AI identification could arise 
from the lack of formal approval or visibility over such tools at the organisational level. 
6.4. Similarly, some respondents expressed concerns over the expectation to incorporate all AI use cases 
into the AI inventory. It was highlighted that such an expectation could capture low-risk or assistive AI 
tools which do not materially impact the FI, leading to unnecessary operational burden. It was also 
highlighted that a comprehensive inventory may not be achievable once the use of AI became 
ubiquitous in the FI. A few respondents also sought clarity on whether AI in the pilot or proof-of-concept 
stages should be captured in the AI inventory. 
MAS’ Response 
6.5. The intent of the AI identification process is to ensure visibility over how AI is used within the FI, as part 
of ensuring effective governance, risk management standards, and controls. The challenges associated 
with identifying embedded AI or shadow AI do not by themselves justify excluding embedded AI or 
shadow AI from the AI identification process in all instances. The assessment of the risks posed by 
embedded AI or shadow AI can only be made by first identifying the sources and nature of such 
embedded AI or shadow AI. Accordingly, MAS’ intent is for the AI identification process to consistently 
identify AI use across all relevant business and functional areas. 
6.6. While an FI should endeavour to identify all instances of embedded AI, MAS recognises that the FI may 
face challenges in doing so. The Guidelines have therefore been revised to clarify that the identification 
process should minimally identify embedded AI in services provided by material third-party service 
providers. This reflects the need for an FI to maintain visibility over material AI dependencies that may 
not be immediately apparent, enabling the FI to identify and manage the risks arising from such 
dependencies. 
6.7. MAS also recognises that it may not be practically possible to ensure complete identification of all AI 
use in practice. The Guidelines have been amended to state that where such constraints exist, the FI 
should assess the risks arising from gaps in visibility and put in place mitigating measures. These could 
include establishing clear policies on permitted and prohibited uses of AI; implementing technical 
controls to restrict access to, or the sharing of sensitive information with, public AI tools where 
appropriate; monitoring for unauthorised AI use; and periodically reinforcing staff awareness of these 
requirements. 
6.8. All AI use cases should be recorded in the AI inventory to the extent possible or practicable. The intent 
of the AI inventory is to serve as the basis upon which an FI performs its risk assessment and applies 
the appropriate risk management controls across the AI life cycle. An FI can implement the AI inventory

in a risk-proportionate manner to manage the operational burden associated with implementing and

Implementation of “robust systems” for identifying AI  
6.9. A few respondents requested clarity on the expectation that “robust systems” be implemented to 
support the process of AI identification. In particular, how such expectations should be guided by the 
principle of proportionality, and whether such systems should include tools which conduct automated 
surveillance to discover shadow AI or un-authorised AI use. A few respondents expressed concerns that 
such surveillance tools may be invasive in nature, and disproportionate to the risks posed.  
MAS’ Response 
6.10. MAS’ intent is that such systems facilitate the process of AI identification, which may otherwise be an

MAS’ Response 
7.9. MAS has considered the feedback and has decided to retain Complexity as a risk dimension. Complexity 
is not intended to be assessed in isolation, nor does it determine risk materiality on a standalone basis. 
Rather, Complexity is one of several dimensions that should be considered in combination, alongside 
factors such as Impact, when determining the overall risk materiality of an AI use case. As clarified in 
paragraph 7.19., FIs have flexibility in determining how individual risk dimensions are combined or 
weighted. 
7.10. MAS recognises that assessments of Complexity may involve judgement and evolve as an FI’s 
understanding of an AI technology develops. This does not diminish the relevance of Complexity as a 
risk dimension. Rather, it underscores the importance of assessing Complexity in the context of the FI’s 
own understanding of, and ability to assess, the technology. 
7.11. Accordingly, an FI that is less familiar with an AI technology, or has more limited ability to understand 
and assess its risks, may reasonably assess Complexity as higher and apply correspondingly more 
rigorous lifecycle controls, such as enhanced testing and pre-deployment review. Where the use of 
third-party AI limits an FI’s visibility into the nature or risks of the AI technology, the FI should similarly 
consider whether this limitation warrants a higher assessment of Complexity. An FI may revise its 
assessment of Complexity, and the corresponding lifecycle controls, as its familiarity with or visibility 
into the AI technology develops over time.

proportionate degree of visibility into updates or changes to third-party AI that could reasonably be 
expected to affect the continued performance, behaviour or risk profile of the AI use case. MAS 
recognises that vendor-driven updates may occur before an FI is able to complete assessments on the 
significance of the updates, and subject significant updates to internal reviews and re-approvals. Where 
such third-party AI relate to use cases of high risk materiality, an FI should implement compensating 
controls such as enhanced monitoring to mitigate the risks associated with significant vendor-driven 
updates proceeding without an FI’s knowledge or prior assessment.

MAS’ Response 
10.77. The intent of footnote 28 is to recognise that an FI may find it necessary to deploy an AI use case in a 
limited fashion in order to establish its suitability. For example, an FI may need to deploy AI to obtain 
sufficient representative real-world data, for the purposes of evaluation and testing. 
10.78. Prior to the start of the pilot or partial deployment, an FI should establish appropriate controls and 
guardrails, guided by the principle that residual risks should remain within the risk appetite of the FI. 
An FI’s policies on the use of pilots or partial deployments should clearly set out such controls and 
guardrails, which may include time‑bound deployment, restricting user access, or other appropriate 
measures. An FI should also ensure that such controls and guardrails are robustly designed and 
implemented, given the possibility for highly autonomous AI to devise ways to evade or circumvent 
these controls. 
10.79. As set out in paragraph 5.19. of the Guidelines, AI use cases assessed as having high risk materiality 
should undergo formal independent validation before deployment; this applies irrespective of whether 
such use cases are pilots or partial deployments.

12. Generative AI and AI Agents 
Discussion of risks 
12.1. One respondent suggested that the discussion of risks posed by Generative AI in Section 1 of the 
Guidelines could inadvertently discourage an FI from investigating or implementing available mitigants 
on the belief that such mitigants would not be acceptable. Another respondent suggested that the 
Guidelines explicitly set out that there are “no restrictions” on the use of Generative AI, or on the types 
of data which may be used, so long as risks are properly understood, assessed, and addressed within a 
FI’s established risk appetite. This respondent also suggested more explicit statements on permissible 
uses of Generative AI, to provide more certainty on whether an FI may deploy Generative AI solutions. 
MAS’ Response 
12.2. The discussion of risks posed by Generative AI and AI agents in Section 1 of the Guidelines is intended 
to alert FIs to how newer AI technologies, specifically Generative AI and AI agents, may amplify risks. 
The intent is to enhance awareness and understanding of such risks, especially by FIs with less 
experience deploying such technologies, and not to discourage or restrict their use.  
12.3. MAS does not intend to restrict the use of any specific AI technology or limit use cases, provided that 
the risks are adequately addressed. An FI should determine how best to deploy AI in a safe and 
responsible manner, taking into account the rigour of its controls, the application of appropriate risk 
mitigants, and its own appetite for risks from AI use.  
12.4. MAS recognises that agentic AI introduces a distinct set of risks that warrant dedicated attention. This 
is given its capacity for autonomous decision-making, tool use, and chaining of actions across multiple 
steps or agents. As the understanding of these risks continues to develop, FIs may refer to relevant 
literature and industry publications to stay abreast of emerging risks and evaluation practices. MAS will 
update the Guidelines to reflect material developments in the Generative AI landscape and supervisory 
expectations at a later stage.

Application of controls 
12.5. A few respondents requested for further guidance on evaluation and testing for Generative AI. In 
particular, they highlighted practical challenges in evaluating such models, noting that traditional 
evaluation methodologies may not be fully applicable. Feedback was also provided on sections that 
could be further adapted for Generative AI, including data management, evaluating and testing

12.6. A few respondents requested for further guidance on the application of lifecycle controls for agentic 
AI, including how risks should be evaluated at the level of individual agents, as well as at the system 
level, taking into account amplification of risks across chains of agents. Additional guidance was 
requested on risk-proportionate monitoring coverage across the action space. One respondent also 
highlighted that some agentic AI systems may autonomously retrieve, combine, or synthesise external 
information, raising distinct considerations such as verifying the provenance of retrieved information, 
assessing reproducibility of outcomes, and documenting reasoning pathways. 
MAS’ Response 
12.7. The Guidelines set out the outcomes and risk management principles that FIs are expected to achieve, 
affording institutions the flexibility to adopt evaluation approaches that are most suited to their specific 
Generative AI or agentic AI application. MAS will not mandate specific testing techniques or minimum 
controls at this time. Nonetheless, MAS has updated relevant sections in the Guidelines to provide 
further illustrations where appropriate, including examples that are relevant in the context of 
Generative AI. 
12.8. With regard to agentic AI, the Guidelines clarify that monitoring should extend, where relevant, to 
information flows and decision-making paths across workflows that use AI, such as reasoning 
processes, actions taken, and tools used. Such monitoring can support the identification of individual 
actions taken across chains of agents, and help pinpoint the sources of specific failures. 
12.9. Given the pace of developments in this area, MAS is taking an iterative approach. In the near term, 
MAS has included the national-level Agentic AI Model Governance Framework published by the 
Infocomm Media Development Authority as a practical reference for managing risks associated with 
agentic and multi-agent systems. Over time, MAS intends to consult the financial sector separately on 
what additional guidance specific to agentic AI would be most helpful, taking into account the sector's

13. Implementation Timeline 
13.1. MAS had proposed a transition period of 12 months after the Guidelines are issued, for FIs to assess 
and implement the Guidelines. 
13.2. Many respondents found the proposed transition period challenging, and requested further time to 
implement the Guidelines, with 18-24 months being the most commonly requested period. Challenges 
cited included the need to source for sufficient expertise/talent to ensure adequate capabilities and 
capacity for AI risk management, the need to identify and apply the Guidelines in respect of third-party 
AI or retrospectively on AI which had already been deployed, or the need to align with Group-level 
policies on AI risk management. 
13.3. Some respondents also proposed a phased transition period, whereby certain aspects of the Guidelines 
would be implemented by 12 months, and additional time taken for full implementation. 
MAS’ Response 
13.4. MAS agrees to allow FIs with more time to fully implement the Guidelines in areas which are more 
resource-intensive.  
13.5. Unless paragraph 2.3. of the Guidelines applies, an FI should meet the expectations in Sections 3 and 4 
of the Guidelines (i.e. establish frameworks for oversight of risks from AI use, and processes for 
identification, inventorisation and risk materiality assessments) by 12 months. Full implementation of 
the Guidelines including Sections 5 and 6 (i.e. the application of lifecycle controls across the FI’s use 
cases, and ensuring adequate capabilities and capacity) should be completed by 24 months. 
Notwithstanding, an FI should apply appropriate lifecycle controls for its high risk use cases as soon as 
possible, and not delay application until the end of the 24-month transition period. 
13.6. An FI may apply basic AI governance policies and procedures for the use of AI if they meet the criteria 
in paragraph 2.3. of the Guidelines, and should complete implementation by 12 months.
