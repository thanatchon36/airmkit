 Foreword
01
Although Artificial Intelligence (AI) has the potential to transform financial services, it must be 
used in a safe and responsible manner. The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) embarked 
on our Responsible AI (RAI) journey in 2018, when we started developing the Fairness, Ethics, 
Accountability and Transparency (FEAT) Principles. In November 2018, MAS introduced the FEAT 
Principles to firms offering financial products and services as a way of providing guidance on the 
responsible use of AI and Data Analytics (AIDA). The greatest challenge for the industry here, as it 
is elsewhere, has been to translate these high-level principles into practice.
As part of Singapore’s National AI Strategy, MAS together with our financial industry partners, has 
created a framework called Veritas. Veritas provides a verifiable way of incorporating the FEAT Principles 
into AIDA solutions to address existing challenges such as the lack of industry methodologies for 
fairness and ethics principles, and also to work towards creating a skilled RAI talent pool. Integrating 
the Veritas methodology will empower financial institutions (FIs) to utilise AI confidently within their 
businesses and to deliver robust, trusted products to their customers. This is fundamental to our end 
goal which is to encourage and foster the adoption of RAI in local and global ecosystems.   
The FEAT Principles were illustrated in Veritas Phases 1 and 2 through use cases such as 
customer marketing, predictive underwriting and credit scoring, which provide implementation 
methodologies for FIs to follow. This whitepaper delves into the practical challenges and lessons 
learned from seven lead FIs on the integration of the Veritas methodology on RAI within their 
organisations and their governance processes. Seven FIs have operationalised the FEAT principles 
by integrating the Veritas methodology within their existing risk-management framework along 
with other FI-specific methodologies. We took a deep dive into their approaches and drew out key 
lessons on how to implement the Veritas methodology in a practical manner.  
Our next step is to build an open-source ecosystem for RAI that is self-sustaining and vibrant. This 
ecosystem will facilitate the pervasive and consistent adoption of RAI principles by FIs and tech 
companies, as well as support a new generation of RAI stewards who are skilled in using RAI tools. 
Additionally, the ecosystem will foster an active community of developers who can implement the 
FEAT Principles through the Veritas open-source ecosystem. Through this collaborative approach, 
we aim to promote RAI practices and ensure that the benefits of AI are shared by all.  
We would like to thank and acknowledge the contribution of the lead members of the consortium 
– BNY Mellon, DBS, HSBC, OCBC, Singlife, Standard Chartered, UOB and Accenture – for the 
development of this whitepaper, which will serve as a resource for FIs commencing the integration 
journey. We thank the members of the Veritas consortium for their feedback on the integration 
journey and welcome any further feedback from the members as well as the public on this 
whitepaper. Finally, we would be happy to hear from you about how we can further enhance the 
Veritas methodology to ensure the responsible use of AI in Singapore and beyond.
Sopnendu Mohanty 
(MAS Chief FinTech Officer)

MAS has been working on Veritas, a multi-phased collaborative project with the financial industry, 
since 2018, with the project currently in its third phase. In Phases 1 and 2, MAS published the 
importance of the FEAT Principles for the financial services sector, and its methodology to 
implement FEAT (the Veritas methodology and toolkit). As part of Phase 3, MAS is working with the 
consortium members on integrating the Veritas methodology within existing risk management 
practices, and documenting potential challenges and lessons learned from these experiences 
which will serve as a reference for future integration cycles in an organisation.
This whitepaper provides an overview of practical challenges and lessons learned from the seven 
lead FIs [BNY Mellon, DBS, HSBC, Singlife, Standard Chartered, OCBC and UOB] on implementing 
and integrating the Veritas methodology on RAI within their organisations and governance 
processes. The integration journey lessons in this study have been documented under four pillars 
– Principles, Governance & Risk Control, Processes & Technology, Culture & Training, and Use-
Case Specific Implementation. The three key takeaways that echoed with the lead FIs in Phase 3
are listed below and detailed in Section 4 of this whitepaper.
1.
There is a need for a consistent and robust RAI framework that can span geographies and
industries, while also being practical to operationalise at scale.
2.
All FIs that participated in this phase utilise a risk-based approach to determine the governance
required for their AI use-cases.
3.
While data-literacy practices are common at all FIs, with many already implementing
e-certifications to increase awareness and promote responsible AI use, there is consensus
on the need for training a new generation of stewards who are groomed on the qualitative
and quantitative aspects of RAI specific to the Financial Services (FS) industry.
Integrating the Veritas methodology will empower FIs to utilise AIDA systems within their businesses 
with confidence and to deliver to their customers robust, trusted products, which is fundamental to 
the Veritas end-goal – i.e., to encourage and foster the adoption of RAI in local and global ecosystems. 
The lessons learned from this phase of Veritas will power our journey to build a self-sustaining, 
evolving, Responsible AI ecosystem that will be built together with the industry.

3.1	 Veritas Key Objectives & Progress 
The MAS-led Veritas consortium developed the Veritas methodology as part of the Singapore 
National AI strategy together with partners from academia and industry, to implement and promote 
four high-level principles (Fairness, Ethics, Accountability and Transparency – the FEAT Principles) 
while handling AI and Data Analytics (AIDA). This helps FIs use and scale AIDA responsibly, while 
mitigating against unforeseen negative consequences. Veritas Phases 1 and 2 aimed to establish 
a methodology for the implementation of the FEAT Principles.
The Veritas methodology was illustrated in Veritas Phases 1 and 2 via use cases such as customer 
marketing, predictive underwriting and credit scoring which provide implementation methodologies 
for FIs to follow. Veritas Phase 2 also involved the development of an open-source toolkit for fairness, 
which enables FIs to implement FEAT on their AI/ML models. The findings and achievements from 
the two phases were published in 20201 and 20212 respectively and are guiding the industry in 
developing and deploying AI-based solutions to serve their customers with confidence. A summary 
of the FEAT Principles and assessment methodologies is provided in Section 3.2.

The Veritas methodology provides FIs with a practical and detailed guide – i.e., a checklist to 
perform a FEAT assessment on their own AIDA models, which they can further adapt and modify 
to suit their organisational requirements. This covers all five steps of the AIDA life cycle – Step 0: 
Principles to Practice; Step 1: Defining System Context and Design; Step 2: Prepare and Input Data; 
Step 3: Build and Validate; and Step 4: Deploy and Monitor. The detailed checklists are published 
as part of the whitepapers at the end of Veritas Phase 2.3 
The methodology provides recommendations on the following aspects for the FIs to consider:
The fairness checklist provides FIs with guidance to assess their AIDA system’s operations with 
the Fairness Principle, and to define their internal foundational set of fairness assessments and 
customisation of assessments for a given AIDA system. The Ethics and Accountability framework 
provides guidance to FIs to move the organisational concepts of ethics to measurable ethical 
practices which can be aligned to the FEAT Principles. The checklist for Transparency covers 
two aspects – external transparency (i.e., what level of transparency should FIs provide to their 
customers/other external stakeholders) and internal transparency (i.e., guidance on how FIs can be 
compliant with relevant internal policies and procedures as well as any regulatory requirements). 
•	 Bring FEAT assessment to scale. 
•	 Define materiality and risk tiers. 
•	 Adopt a risk-based governance approach for application of FEAT Principles to different 
use cases. 
•	 Integrate FEAT with existing risk management practices.

i.	
Principles, Governance & Risk Control 
	 	
This pillar aims to capture the integration insights from the alignment of an organisation’s 
RAI framework with its own core values and principles. This is a strategic-level pillar to 
understand the RAI policy at the organisation-wide level, and its integration with the 
existing risk management measures within the organisation. The RAI Principles will 
dictate within the organisation its RAI policy that will in turn determine the enterprise 
governance frameworks. Within this pillar, FIs describe the roles, responsibilities, and 
accountability of all stakeholders in the organisation. In addition, this pillar captures 
lessons learned by FIs during risk-tiering, how materiality features into the risk-tiering as 
well as their considerations for making the organisation‘s policy and control measures 
commensurate with risk levels. Finally, this pillar highlights the measures of progress 
in rolling out AIDA governance to ensure that the organisations can keep pace with the 
evolving AI technologies. 
ii.	 Processes & Technology
	 	
This is another use-case agnostic pillar to document the lessons learned and experiences of the 
FIs during the setup of the Operations (Ops) team – the structure, process and documentation 
involved. Processes flow from the risk-tiering and governance frameworks identified and 
established in the organisation (with lessons learned from this covered under Pillar 1). 
Examples of processes discussed include reporting processes and escalation processes. The 
processes pillar also contains documentation on the best practices within each organisation 
– i.e., a Responsible AI Guidebook that each organisation has started to build for itself. It also 
covers the experiences from the overall tech platform for data, ML, MLOps and how these are 
integrated with the Enterprise Risk Management Framework (ERMF).  
iii.	 Culture & Training
	 	
Within this pillar, organisations aim to describe their journey identifying the skills required 
for different RAI roles. The FIs will discuss their experiences in setting up the training 
processes (frequency, duration, number of courses etc) as well as the measures taken 
to ensure that the employees are constantly learning. This section would also explore 
how FIs explain best practices documented in their processes to the practitioners. This 
encompasses an organisation-wide effort from the strategic to operational levels to 
understand RAI principles – i.e., training at all levels as required for them to perform their 
duties, and their role in contributing practically to RAI processes & technology.
iv. Use-Case Specific Implementation 
	 	
This pillar focuses on highlighting insights on the RAI integration through the lens of the 
chosen use case. The FIs would consolidate their learnings on the AIDA model lifecycle.

3.4	 Audience for this Whitepaper
This whitepaper is intended to serve as a reference or baseline for audiences from FIs, tech and 
regulatory bodies on integrating Responsible AI within an organisation across different levels from 
leadership to  developers. Those who will benefit from this whitepaper include representatives 
from FIs – C-Suite level to practitioners – such as heads of AI/data departments and data scientists/
data engineers.  
The first three pillars are use-case agnostic and fall within the typical policy, process and risk 
control pyramids that exist in FIs in some form today. This whitepaper represents a step towards 
capturing the lessons learned as FIs operationalise their internal AI frameworks. To summarise, 
RAI Principles dictate an organisation’s RAI policy, and this includes policies for risk-tiering i.e., 
materiality and a library of potential risk controls. The organisation’s policy in turn would shape the 
enterprise governance and the roles and responsibilities of all stakeholders from board members to 
data-science practitioners. The organisation processes – i.e., escalations or day-to-day operations 
fall within the RAI processes layer, stem from the risk library and are commensurate with the 
materiality of the use cases. The best practices within each FI i.e., a FI specific RAI guidebook 
would be very useful for training employees on the organisation’s governance frameworks and 
processes. This is captured in Figure 3.3.

i)	
Principles, Governance & Risk Control: 
	 	
In this pillar, we highlight the key lessons learned by FIs while integrating the Veritas 
methodology with their own organisation principles and how this influences the set-up of 
their governance framework. There are three key aspects that are consistent across all the FIs.   
a)	 Three lines of defence (LODs) defined
	 	
FIs are aligned in delineating roles and responsibilities  for all stakeholders to ensure 
model risk management. Typically, and as described in the case studies, most FIs have 
three lines of defence with delineated responsibilities associated with specific roles 
that are independent in nature to overcome conflicts of interest in decision-making. 
BNY Mellon, for example, stated the following in its case study: “While roles and 
responsibilities in the process depend on the size and complexity of the organisational 
structure, an organisation should create three lines of defence: model-owner oversight, 
model risk management and internal audit.” Similarly, DBS has its own version of the three 
LODs with roles and responsibilities “defined for model owners, developers, materiality 
approvers, deployment approvers and independent reviewers with appropriate seniority 
specified.” Standard Chartered Bank has specific roles “defined within our RAI Standard 
in terms of authority to execute first-line, second-line and third-line responsibilities.” 
	 	
All the FIs stated that integrating the Veritas methodology with existing governance 
frameworks typically involved expanding the assessment scope to cover all AIDA models 
– i.e., it needs more resources whose roles are defined. They also noted that qualified 
resources are required to retrospectively apply governance controls to currently 
deployed AI models and newly deployed AI models when all are exponentially scaling 
the use of AI/ML at the same time as developing/operationalising the AI governance 
framework. As discussed in the BNY Mellon case study, the triad of people, process 
and technology ties very closely with data, processing and skilled teams, and leads to a 
functioning governance framework. This triad of people, process and technology is also 
central to UOB’s Data Ethics Governance Framework as described in its case study and 
from this paper can be broadly said to be applicable and relevant to RAI Frameworks.  
b)	 Risk-based governance approach 
	 	
There was unanimous agreement by all FIs on the importance of risk-tiering and the need 
to assess the materiality of a use case to determine the level of governance required to 
prevent harm to customers, employees and the organisation. This is exemplified in the 
individual FI case studies where firms adopted similar methodologies to ensure that use 
cases with the highest materiality were under stricter governance, and where other use 
cases had a baseline governance level with recommendations commensurate with risk 
levels. HSBC describes its model risk management strategy in detail in its case study: 
“Models and model use cases are assigned a model risk materiality rating (MRMR) based 
on the impact. The group impact scale is used to assess the plausible impact on the 
business within the next 12 months.” Similarly, OCBC also employs a risk-based approach 
in governance of AI use cases stating “A key principle was to take a risk-based approach 
where the depth of validation of controls required for an AIDA system are commensurate 
with the level of potential harm arising from unintended biases from the system.”

c)	 Adapting global policies within the organization frameworks 
	 	
Typically, FIs operate globally and so their governance frameworks are a culmination of 
aspects of policies spanning different geographies, such as MAS’s FEAT Principles and the 
Hong Kong Monetary Authority’s (HKMA) High-level Principles on AI. Thus, they need to 
refer to multiple policies to draft a framework for their organisation to remain compliant. 
The study by DBS alludes to this challenge and highlights the need for a “consistent 
industry-wide governance approach.” Standard Chartered Bank’s case study also lists its 
10 principles, including FEAT, and how those are mapped at a granular level to more than 
one national policy (MAS’s Veritas and HKMA’s BDAI guidelines). This brings out the need 
for the creation of robust, industry standards which can be applied at a global level and 
used by FIs to develop their AIDA governance frameworks. In the upcoming work with 
the FIs, MAS and Accenture are planning to materialise this objective and develop a 
consortium to build “living” RAI standards, which are global in nature.  
ii)	 Processes & Technology: 
	 	
This pillar is use-case agnostic and captures the lessons documented by the FIs on their 
own organisation’s best practices for operational efficiencies while ensuring Responsible 
AI use. There are two common themes across FIs here: 
a)	 Innovative processes for RAI documentation to keep pace with the 
evolving technology   
	 	
A common suggestion from FIs in keeping pace with constantly evolving AI technology 
is that the frameworks, processes and operations need to be flexible enough to 
accommodate these timely updates while not stifling innovation. The processes also

6.1.1	 Overview
Frameworks & Foundations
Our Approach
Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) introduce new risks and raise new questions 
about how to achieve optimal results. Our teams took a detailed approach and created a 
prioritisation plan to execute Project Veritas efficiently. Our plan’s design was driven by our 
approach and a framework of AI and ML based on three key pillars: data, processing and skilled 
teams under a functioning governance.  
Data is the foundation of all AI and ML. When creating traditional technology platforms, one usually 
starts with a beta version, uses subsets of data, and adjusts to create the next version. However, 
in creating AI/ML solutions, these legacy approaches simply do not work. An accurate AI model 
requires all of the data at the start, or at the very least a very large and representative subset so the 
models will work appropriately and find the correlating features. Often, specific identifier fields 
can be tokenised provided the tokens can be correlated.  
Due to the significant demands of data in AI/ML models, there must be sufficient processing power 
to process it all at speed. A distributed computing environment is required to enable productive 
processing speeds, and often the best option is an elastic environment that allows many specific 
tasks to share a majority of the processing power for short periods. 
Most importantly, data and processing space rely on the expertise of the human team, and the 
team’s competence depends on many factors: understanding the problem context, familiarity with 
the data resources, and experience with existing non-AI human intuition-driven approaches. Also 
critical is the scientific method used: the objective is to propose hypotheses (i.e., we can forecast 
x, given data y..z. ŷ = b0 + b1x). It is critical that the team can create an effective hypothesis, and 
structure its execution, data and processing so that the model will be effective. 
While the team must start with a solid hypothesis, it must do so under a dual lens. Ultimately, the 
objective must be to attempt to disprove the hypothesis. This can feel contrary to the business 
objectives and requires understanding scientific principles to support the hypothesis and the 
anti-hypothesis. If teams are not focused on both the hypothesis and the anti-hypothesis, they 
waste time and effort trying to justify the often-sunk cost of creating a sub-par model. Teams must 
be vigilant, offsetting a natural inclination to defend a hypothesis by creating candid, unflinching 
documentation and requiring peer reviews of what was proven and disproven. 
Finally, there must be skilled functional governance expertise. The team that creates the models 
must understand the law, policies, ethics and appropriateness of the goal of the model, the data 
that is deemed appropriate for the goal, the approvals necessary for data stewards and providers, 
and the records that must be maintained for future audit and review.

6.1.2	 (AI/ML) Governance
Vigilance & Methods for Mitigation 
If vigilance is not put in place, BFD issues may creep in unnoticed. Existing data resources may 
reflect historical inequities, and the data may include features about people or events that can 
lead to biased treatment. This data can be an essential input for detecting useful signals, such as 
correlations (e.g., Granger causality) for predictive analytics. Yet, when this data is fed blindly into 
AI models, these models may yield biased results. 
There are oft-practised methods to address bias or signs of discrimination such as inaction, feature 
prohibition, unaware modelling, demographic parity and mastering data. Firstly, a team can, and 
sometimes does, take no action. Blindly following the data will likely perpetuate and create a built-
in bias.  
Another strategy is removing prohibited features in the data, creating a fairness-through-
unawareness strategy. This is largely ineffective, uninformed and fails to address redundant and/
or correlated proxy attributes that are highly correlated with them. Moreover, it deletes potentially 
relevant and critically important signals for the analysis which prevents the use of data science 
itself to combat bias proactively.  
Group unaware modelling is another strategy. One can implement the same threshold and/or 
strategy for all demographics in a data set. However, this can ignore insightful legitimate variances 
between groups as this strategy would price them the same. Unfortunately, even with the best 
group-unaware models, this approach may still discriminate between groups because underlying 
distribution differences are ignored. 
One can also tweak algorithms to achieve demographic parity. ML models may be proactively 
adjusted so that different thresholds are used for different groups such that the positive rate is the 
same across all groups (i.e., the percentage of instances classified in a certain direction is the same). 
Our team’s preference is another strategy. The teams that operate and manage the AI/ML model 
must understand and master the data used to enforce equal opportunity – a strategy that is optimal 
for comprehensively managing bias. Moreover, ML algorithms may be adjusted so that different 
thresholds are used for different groups such that the true positive rate is the same across all groups 
(i.e., performance of the classifier is the same across groups). We find that this approach produces 
the best and most useful data science results, which then leads to more optimal decision-making. 
Once this is complete, it is necessary to do a materiality assessment and risk-tiering.

Materiality Assessment
Materiality assessment and risk-tiering are necessary to comply with the Model Risk Governance 
(MRG) processes for models. Risk related with the use of particular AIDA factors can result in both 
financial loss and reputational harm to an institution. 
Model-tiering stems from the inherent risk of the model. Managing AIDA must first be commensurate 
with the materiality of the model. In general, three aspects should be taken into consideration 
when assessing model materiality: complexity, capital and liquidity, and model interaction:    
•	 Complexity: Models can range in complexity from simple decision trees to neural 
networks. The more complex the model, the higher its tier. This may be due to a lack 
of transparency in the model’s approach, a network effect whereby the model relies on 
many segments, or a unique approach that is not easily benchmarked.  
•	 Capital and liquidity: Capital and liquidity impact a model’s rate of failure, which may be 
due to the short – or long-term effects of a capital position or liquidity. Reputational risk 
falls under this category. The more significant and immediate the effect is, the higher the 
tier it should have.
•	 Model Interaction: Tiering must further reflect how models interact, such that if a model 
provides a principal input to a downstream model, its support for processes that affect a 
firm’s capital, liquidity, profitability and reputation should be considered at least as great 
as the downstream model.
Additional aspects that may be taken into consideration for tiering are automatism between 
models’ output and the decision-making process (reliance on the model’s output), and the number 
of processes supported by the model or model exposure.

Risk-Tiering Strategies 
AIDA models should follow standard model-risk policies and procedures. Structuring the process 
and the layers of documentation depend on the size and complexity of the firm. However, the 
general principle is that there should be three tiers of normative acts: 
•	 Tier 1: Policies – The board or committees should define high-level key controls on model 
risk management. Breaches of the policy should be reported back to the approving 
body. Moreover, the body approving policy should also oversee models with a high-level 
risk appetite. 
•	 Tier 2: Procedures – Documentation must define the practical implementation of 
policies, and this documentation should be approved at the chief risk officer (CRO) or 
head of model risk level. Breaches of the procedure should be reported back to the 
approving body. Finally, an approving procedure should also oversee KRIs/KPIs related 
to model risk.
•	 Tier 3: Templates – Documentation should standardise how the model document and 
validation reporting works. An approving body should have adequate control to promote 
consistent template adoption.

Stakeholders & Required Roles   
Roles & Responsibilities 
While roles and responsibilities in the process depend on the size and complexity of the 
organisational structure, an organisation should create three lines of defence: model-
owner oversight, model risk management and internal audit.  
Model-owner oversight is required to support efficacy. Senior staff should oversee model 
owners, users, developers and the business risk committees, and are responsible for 
ensuring a business area is aware of the risk created by its models and is managing that 
risk. Model owners, on the other hand, should manage the risk from specific models so 
that proper development, validation and use takes place. For this to be effective, the 
model owner should have the experience to understand a model’s approach and proper 
use. The model owner will also require supporting owners – modelers, developers and 
technical experts familiar with industry best practice for modelling methodologies.  
Model risk management (MRM) is a second line of defence and has the primary responsibility 
of implementing the policies, procedures and standards on behalf of an oversight board. 
It should be staffed by those with the quantitative background and industry experience 
necessary to identify and consider options to mitigate model risk. Specifically, MRM 
should: 
•	 Monitor compliance with applicable regulatory guidance, rules and regulations. 
•	 Establish and maintain a consistent model risk-management framework across the company. 
•	 Train and provide templates to model owners, users and developers to promote consistent 
application of this policy across the firm.  
•	 Provide a process to inventory and maintain the inventory of the company’s models to 
enable reporting on model risk including periodically reviewing the inventory for obsolete/
unused models and encouraging their retirement or suspension. 
•	 Execute the tiering process for models.  
•	 Establish the effective challenge of models, consisting of critical analysis by objective 
informed parties who can identify model risks and influence appropriate mitigation options.  
•	 Through validation activities, identify and guide the management of model risk in the company.  
•	 Report to the board and committees. 
Within the second line of defence, it may be necessary to set up a committee that oversees the 
MRM process. The committee might be empowered to resolve issues before escalation to the 
board, and acts as a check on the validation group.  
Internal audit is the third line of defence. This function assesses the effectiveness of internal 
controls by providing independent, risk-based assurance reviews designed to identify controls, 
risk mitigants, control gaps and opportunities to improve efficiency. Internal audit is expected to 
periodically assess compliance with this policy, following its practices and methods. Internal audit 
does not revalidate individual models although it may sample models in its analysis of the framework. 
A board may set up key model risk-management principles and document the appetite for model risk.

Team Demographics & Lessons Learned  
The BNY Mellon operations team has learned many lessons throughout our Responsible 
Artificial Intelligence (RAI) process. First, smaller teams of experts were found to be more 
impactful. Smaller teams allow for quick actions and fewer coordination issues. This 
reduces the overall time for a model to go into production, which improves the “time to 
value” for a data science project. 
We have found that creating “Core Teams” of one data scientist, one data engineer and one 
business analyst, who is a domain expert and understands the underlying transactional data, 
is optimal. These “Core Teams” can then be augmented by support staff comprised of other 
data scientists and engineers as well as cloud experts. The benefit is that there is less overhead 
around coordination and more focus on core engineering buildout and experimentation, 
though success hinges on identifying the right key talent. More importantly, team members 
enjoy experimentation via different models.  
A multi-disciplinary team reduces the risk of ending up with a model that reflects the biases 
and assumptions of developers only. A well-rounded team is recommended as a requisite for 
an AIDA people setup.
Developing Multi-disciplinary Teams   
In order to create a comprehensive multi-disciplinary team, there are specific roles to

It is also beneficial to have executive sponsors who understand the technical aspects

Assessing Impact    
Having clear business objectives is the key to developing a fit-for-purpose AIDA system. 
Business objectives are measured quantitatively using model performance. Acceptance 
criteria are set based on iterative discussions between model developers and SMEs, who 
consider various types of potential risk and business benefit according to the model risk 
rating. The trade-off between model performance and RAI aspects such as fairness should 
be decided by in-depth discussion with SMEs and AIDA system-owners.
Before we discuss the trade-off between model performance and fairness, a fundamental 
step is to make sure that fairness is defined and assessed in a suitable and relevant context. 
Unlike other types of risk, fairness is a special topic which is not fully well defined, and it 
could be use-case specific.
There are four key considerations in performing a relevant fairness assessment:
a.	 Selection of protected variable(s) – There may be more than one protected variable 
with each covering different business aspects. For the current use case, three protected 
variables are considered, namely country, segment and class. 
b.	 Identify the privileged/unprivileged group(s)6 – Privileged and unprivileged groups 
can be examined based on a statistical representation or historical bias (not exclusive) 
in conjunction with SME knowledge and expertise. Representation bias occurs when 
certain groups are underrepresented in the data set. Thus, from a statistical point of 
view, the least-represented group(s) is prone to be the unprivileged group, and vice versa 
for the privileged group. One the other hand, the privileged group could be identified 
based on historical bias. For example, highly educated groups of individuals would have 
privilege in terms of credit scoring from historical records based on manual approval 
before utilising the AI and ML models. The identified privileged and unprivileged groups 
should be validated using business domain knowledge from SMEs. For this use case, 
the privileged group for the country-protected variable is selected based on statistical 
representation, while for segment and class, privileged groups are identified based on 
historical bias and business understanding. 
c.	 Group fairness – Many fairness assessment toolkits are developed with one commonly 
used assumption to simplify the privileged/unprivileged groups into a binary-class 
grouping, meaning grouping multiple minor groups into one unprivileged group. Different 
fairness metrics are calculated to compare the unprivileged with the privileged in terms 
of benefits and harms. However, this binary-class grouping of privileged/unprivileged 
groups may introduce additional bias and the fairness conclusion may be misleading. In 
the current use case, different sub-groups are compared with the identified privileged 
group, so fairness metrics are calculated for different privileged/unprivileged pairs across 
different protected variables.

d.	 Primary fairness metric(s) – There are around 18 fairness metrics in the Veritas toolkit 
and customised fairness metrics can be developed based on use-case specific fairness 
objectives. Among all these fairness metrics, one or more primary fairness metric(s) are 
selected to make the fairness conclusion. The Veritas toolkit provides a FairnessTree 
to help users automatically select the primary fairness metric based on three fairness 
considerations, namely fairness priority, fairness concern and fairness impact. The primary 
fairness metric should be selected to align with the fairness objectives, and the trade-off 
analysis with business objectives should be handled with thoughtful considerations.
Giving a fairness assessment conclusion requires deliberate considerations. This brings several

1)	 How to synthesise different pair fairness results? Simple methods include giving different

2)	 How to conduct the trade-off between model performance metric and the synthesised 
fairness results? Simple trade-off analysis for a privileged/unprivileged pair may not work

There are mainly two risk-monitoring and governance processes that are implemented: a central

While statistical models follow a rigorous process to define their risk, machine learning models do 
not. Though the models may have good overall accuracy, they are not accurate for all data-points, 
and their accuracy can change with retrained models. A different approach is required to handle 
the risk in model errors. 
The explainability of machine learning models allows for simpler models such as linear regression. 
On the other hand, logistics regression and decision trees have algorithm-specific explainability, 
and the majority of the models cannot be mathematically explained as these use a model-
agnostic method. 
Shapley values are also a widely used approach from cooperative game theory that come with 
desirable properties. The core idea behind Shapley value-based explanations of machine learning 
models is to use fair allocation results from cooperative game theory to allocate credit for a model’s 
output f(x) among its input features. One of the fundamental properties of Shapley values is that 
they always sum up to the difference between the game outcome when all players are present 
and the game outcome when no players are present. The “game” is the prediction task for a single 
instance of the dataset. The “gain” is the actual prediction for this instance minus the average 
prediction for all instances. The “players” are the feature values of the instance that collaborate to 
receive the result. The Shapley value is the average marginal contribution of a feature value across 
all possible coalitions.  
For machine learning models this means that SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) values of all the 
input features will always sum up to the difference between the baseline (expected) model output 
and the current model output for the prediction being explained. The difference between the 
prediction and the average prediction is fairly distributed among the feature values of the instance. 
The Shapley value allows contrastive explanations. Instead of comparing a prediction to the average 
prediction of the entire dataset, you could compare it to a subset or even to a single data point. 
There is however an impact of interpretability. In situations where the law requires explainability, 
the Shapley value could be a good solution as it is model-agnostic, is based on a solid theory and 
distributes the effects fairly.  
Given a result, Shapley values can be used to explain the impact of each feature to contribute to 
the result. More importantly, this can also be used for a simplified what-if analysis for a specific 
data point, which gives an intervention solution for the model, and which must be added to the 
solution. Prominent subsets of data must be explained using Shapley values, giving an overall 
perspective of the model. 
There is immense value in interpretability for the end-user. First, a user can see the result and 
discern if it makes sense based on the combination of the feature values. An SME who uses the 
model can judge whether the model’s result is logical for a datapoint and can accept the result on 
a case-by-case basis. In this way, we transfer the responsibility of the model to an informed user 
with the goal that the end-user understands and accepts the responsibility of using the model and 
all the impacts that come with it.

6.2.1	 Overview
The volume, velocity and variety of data are increasing at an unprecedented rate, with technology, 
regulatory views and societal norms also evolving at pace. With these advancements and newer 
varieties of data, numerous inferences can be derived by organisations to add value to customers

including customers, employees, regulators and society at large, are becoming mindful of the 
risks involved in how organisations collect, analyse and use their data, and have less tolerance for 
mismanagement or irresponsible data use than before. 
At DBS, we commenced our Responsible Data Use journey in 2018 and worked towards establishing 
a simple yet effective framework to ensure any proposed data use was responsible and aligned to 
our organisational values, without compromising our efficiency or use of data.

in the organisation’s DNA, supported by an assessment tool, defined roles and responsibilities, 
comprehensive training, a bank-wide standard and appropriate senior management oversight. 
As we started to leverage and scale our use of Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning (AI/ML), 
we also ensured that we did so responsibly. This was the start of our AI industrialisation journey 
that coincided with our engagement with the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) and the 
development of the FEAT (Fairness, Ethics, Accountability, Transparency) Principles.
A Holistic Approach to Responsible Data Use 
DBS looks at responsible data use holistically to retain our stakeholders’ trust and manage data 
risks as we continue to maximise value from our data. Our responsible data-use framework ensures 
that we address three core questions through our processes and practices: 
1.	 Can we use the data? This is through our already established and ongoing data 
management, security and compliance processes, tools, practices and operating models 
to ensure data is owned, secured, accessible, lawful and managed. 
2.	 Should we use the data? Strongly aligned to our organisational values, the PURE 
framework addresses the dimensions of our data use and remains sensitive to changing 
social norms and cultural nuances across core markets.
3.	 How we use the data? The governance approach for our AI/ML solutions ensures 
governance commensurate with the risks posed, and follows closely the MAS FEAT 
Principles of fairness, transparency and accountability. This part of our Responsible Data 
Use framework will be addressed in this document.

Scope
There are varied views at a macro level on what constitutes an AI solution that needs to be 
governed. Regulatory publications consider a system consisting of multiple AI and non-AI models, 
human interventions and rules, etcetera, to arrive at decisions. Developing and operationalising a 
governance approach for such end-to-end solutions is not easy and hence it is also important that 
we define what will fall in and out of scope for consistent application across the bank.
MAS has also highlighted the need to have a standard way to determine the scope for governance 
in its recently published information paper titled Implementation of Fairness Principles in Financial 
Institutions’ Use of Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning”.
At DBS, the following factors are considered to determine the scope for AI/ML governance:
(i) Solution is built using first-principles knowledge/business rules/logic; AND,
(ii)	 Solution is exploratory in nature or is not intended to be used in production. 
All solutions which do not meet the above criteria will be considered to be in scope for governance.
Materiality Assessment – To Determine Level of Governance 
Solutions identified as in scope are assessed using a rubric to determine the materiality of its risk. 
The derived materiality score subsequently determines the extent of governance applied. Those 
identified to have a possible high adverse impact will require higher governance than solutions 
with a low adverse impact, ensuring governance is commensurate with impact. Thus, low-impact 
solutions are also not subjected to unnecessary measures that reduce their time to market. 
Materiality is assessed based on the below dimensions: 
1.	 Degree of adverse impact: Severity and scale of any unjustified adverse impact by the 
solution output, when used in decision-making, to customers, employees and/or the 
bank. Some of the dimensions considered are, but are not limited to:
•	 Benignity of the content.
• Possibility of withholding product/service or causing disadvantage.
• Estimated financial loss based on a threshold. 
•	 Possibility of litigation, regulatory warnings, administrative penalties, etc. 
2.	 Level of solution autonomy: Extent of human involvement in the use of solution output 
in decision-making. 
The materiality assessment approach helps to quickly, consistently and appropriately assess a 
solution’s materiality across our analytics teams and diverse use cases. It has been developed after 
carefully considering different assessment dimensions, qualitative and quantitative measures, 
and risk impact categories. Our key learning was that adding more dimensions, measurements 
and granular segmentation of risk categories added complexity, subjectivity and employee toil 
with no measurable value in determining the level of governance required for the solution. We will 
iteratively review and revise the approach as we gain more experience and insights through its 
continuous application.

Use Case Specific Lessons Learned and Recommendations 
•	 The contextual marketing use cases, by their very nature, do not restrict customers 
from accessing any product or service and pose no risk of unjustified systematic 
disadvantage. It is a time-tested and accepted way of marketing products and services 
across industries. An industry-wide view of the types of risks to be governed and 
minimum controls to be in place for such low-materiality use cases is needed, without 
having to apply fully fledged frameworks.
•	 Industry-wide standards are needed on how various metrics for fairness are to be selected 
and thresholds set, the absence of which can show varied results across organisations 
due to differing interpretations and tools. 
•	 Biases can originate from systemic factors like the behaviour of customers towards a 
specific product (e.g., those with higher income have a greater chance of signing up for 
complex investment products) or from the model development process itself (sample 
selection, algorithm and/or data used by the model). The current fairness metric and 
logic suggested for this use case measure both types of biases. Efforts should be made 
to determine ways to measure only the unfairness created by the model development 
process itself, so that targeted actions can be taken to minimise such biases.
•	 For most contextual marketing use cases, propensity models are not channel-specific, 
with a single propensity model being used to deliver marketing communication across 
channels. Though in the above illustration, to determine the fairness metric, the model 
outcome has been determined as “not selective” to predicted positives, exclusions 
will be applied depending on the communication channels. These exclusions will 
not contribute to unfairness as they can either be justified using internal policies or 
processes and/or involve customers’ freedom of choice to opt out.
•	 For the marketing use case, it was relatively easy to apply the Veritas toolkit as it is 
tailored for contextual marketing use cases. However, it is not clear how much effort will 
be required for another use case for which there is no tailored template within the toolkit.  
•	 Clear documentation of the workflow, definitions and logics used in the tool is needed 
for developers to contribute to the continuous development of the open-source tool.
•	 Extension of the Veritas package to support non-Python-based pipelines or models like 
Spark will enable wider adoption. 
•	 Detailed quantitative and qualitative trainings are needed for analytics and business 
teams on the full fairness methodology and toolkit, specifically on choosing metrics, 
thresholds, bias detection, etcetera as currently it is not straightforward for models 
using regression, recommendation, etc. 
•	 Product-buying patterns vary seasonally and/or over time. Progressive development of 
a model based on the availability of additional data sources in an agile manner, the 
adoption of the latest AI/ML algorithms and frequent retraining help to improve model 
performance. For any solution, irrespective of its materiality, processes and platforms 
should support regular monitoring to identify and remediate any data drift and 
deterioration in model performance.

•	 In case the thresholds cannot be met, an assessment of the reasons, possible trade-offs 
and next steps should be evaluated and documented. 
•	 Effective partnership between business, analytics and technology/platform teams in 
creating a roadmap and building tools to support the end-to-end AI/ML lifecycle and 
issue-resolution significantly improves the time to market. 
Other Lessons Learned and Recommendations
•	 Consistent industry-wide governance approach: It is important to establish a consistent 
and pragmatic baseline across the industry on what is in scope, how materiality is assessed 
and necessary basic hygiene governance controls. Methodologies, tools, etcetera can 
further be adopted for more enhanced governance based on materiality. For example, 
contextual marketing is a case study used to demonstrate the Veritas Fairness Assessment 
Methodology in the Veritas whitepaper; the use case, by its very nature, does not restrict 
customers from accessing any product or service and hence has a limited systematic 
disadvantage. It is important to have a view on how such low-materiality use cases are to be 
governed without applying fully fledged frameworks. 
•	 Incremental vs. big bang approach: The emerging and complex nature of AI/ML calls for 
an equally adaptive and nimble governance approach to consider potential risks which may 
have no precedent (we do not know what we do not know). A measured approach will 
help to avoid any future rework/restructuring due to rapid advancements in technology, 
regulatory views, etcetera across jurisdictions. Start small, take one step at a time and 
build incrementally, without using elaborate and rigid frameworks, to ensure organisations 
understand the risks they need to address.
•	 Competitive disadvantage vis-à-vis tech organisations: Governance needs to be balanced 
with the materiality of the impact of the AI-driven decision/action on the impacted party and 
time to market to avoid slowing down innovative applications of AI/ML. We are cognisant 
that much of the early guidance in this area would instruct future binding regulation. 
While this is only a natural progression, it is important that this is done in a manner where 
competitiveness is considered both at home and in the regions in which the banks operate. 
•	 Treatment of complex, ensemble and dynamically changing solutions or models: 
Organisations are starting to adopt complex use cases and solutions where numerous, multi-
layered and dynamically trained models contribute to the overall outcome. Governance 
of these use cases and model groups will need a non-traditional approach and guidance. 
This also holds true for solutions using a combination of model outputs, rules and human 
judgement in the final decision. 
•	 Talent shortage: It is difficult to find people with both governance and AI/ML experience 
or interest, and to understand and translate the emerging industry or regulatory views 
into approaches suitable for operationalisation. Extensive options for structured learnings, 
discussion forums, etcetera are needed to bring the available people up to speed on various 
principles, requirements, methodologies and tools.

Interpretability is also especially important for machine learning models. Many machine learning 
algorithms have been labelled as “black box” models because of their inscrutable inner workings. 
Such algorithms of machine learning include gradient-boosted ensembles (GBM), artificial neural 
networks (ANN) and random forests, among many others. What makes these models accurate is 
what makes their predictions difficult to understand: they are very complex. This is a fundamental 
trade-off. Unfortunately, more accuracy almost always comes at the expense of interpretability, and 
interpretability is crucial for business adoption, model documentation, regulatory oversight and 
human acceptance and trust. Interpretable, fair and transparent models are encouraged as part of 
the oversight of the use of AI-ML models.
If AI/ML systems make mistakes or have undesired consequences, it is not acceptable to use as 
an excuse that “the algorithm did it”. HSBC has a two-tier approach to dealing with this problem. 
In certain use cases (e.g., pricing decisions) that directly impact customers or are required by 
regulators, the methods with transparency and interpretability should be used. Sometimes this will 
mean that modellers may select not to use the most predictive deep-learning algorithms but instead 
settle on more basic machine learning algorithms to enable transparency. For other use cases (e.g., 
marketing message prioritisation) that are less material in terms of their business impact and risk, 
less model transparency or interpretability may be tolerated, and focus should be put more on the 
model accuracy and test of its “fitness for purpose”. However, even in scenarios where complex 
models are allowed, HSBC recommends industry-leading approaches such as Shapley values to 
interpret the model results, and these are embedded in the internally developed modelling tools.  
•	 Act consistently with HSBC values: We have seen use cases where data exists to perform 
a specific model or analysis, but after reviewing whether that analysis will align with our 
values and societal good, we have decided not to progress with that analysis.
•	 Address unfair bias and decision-making: Models must avoid using restricted class 
variables explicitly in the model, and are checked for implicit bias against these variables. 
In some cases, this has led us to rework models which showed a biased outcome, leading 
to additional work and sometimes less predictive models.

To assess if systematic disadvantage could be introduced by the system, the project teams are 
required to consider the following vis-à-vis the use case: 
•	 Does the model impact the customer’s eligibility for products/services? 
•	 Will a wrong prediction potentially impact the customer’s long-term reputation? 
•	 Does a wrong prediction potentially have a negative psychological effect or result in 
physical harm to the customer? 
As a provider of economic resources, we consider the customer’s ability to access our offerings as 
a key fairness consideration. If the model is used as an input to determine an individual’s ability to 
access a product or service (e.g., by controlling eligibility or pricing), it is deemed a potential source 
of systematic disadvantage. These models should be treated more stringently than models that only 
support the promotion or awareness for the service (e.g., chatbots or marketing models). 
If it is deemed that the model does not cause systematic disadvantage, AI Lab does not require further 
review of the trade-off between model performance and model fairness. Being able to maintain a 
lightweight process for non-harmful models is a key factor in making sure that the organisation can 
develop and deploy such models quickly without incurring unnecessary overheads. 
If the model is assessed as having the potential to cause systematic disadvantage, then the trade-
off between performance and fairness needs to be analysed. Working together with the business 
process-owner, data scientists first need to identify all possible protected groups. For each of these 
groups, they are required to determine the acceptable level of fairness (according to the selected 
fairness metric), and the maximum acceptable degradation in model performance. To guide this 
process, AI Lab requires the team to answer several questions to demonstrate a shared understanding 
of the fairness analysis, mitigation measures and trade-offs as part of the project documentation: 
1. 	 What are the fairness objectives for the system?  
This requires the team to articulate the source of any possible systematic disadvantage.  
2. 	 Does the data used by the system contain properties that may impact the system fairness? 
This requires the team to perform a comprehensive data exploration and document any inherent 
biases or gross imbalances in the data that could contribute to systematic disadvantages. 
3. 	 How is this impact mitigated? 
The team will need to articulate the mitigation plan, possibly using a combination of measures – 
e.g., rebalancing the data, employing explainable models, using fairer measures of performance, 
and tracking a suitable fairness metric. These mitigation measures should be meaningfully 
demonstrated through experiments where possible. 
4. 	 What personal attributes are relevant to the use case? What are the justifications for using 
	
these personal attributes within the model? 
This requires clear articulation as to why a personal attribute is used in training the model – e.g., a 
feature could be a strong predictor of the target variable, without which the business use case may 
not be compelling. This discussion should also encompass scenarios where personal attributes 
may be omitted from the model, but could still be needed to track fairness during model validation 
to avoid assuming “fairness through unawareness”.

Step 2: Development & Deployment 
Once the fairness assessment has been approved by the business process-owner, the constraints 
required for performance and fairness are registered within the OCBC Model Management Platform. 
As data scientists develop their models iteratively, the model metrics, including performance and 
fairness scores (generated by the OCBC fork of the Veritas toolkit), are submitted to the platform. 
Based on the submitted results, the model management platform shows whether a trade-off 
between fairness and performance is possible without breaking the given constraints in Table 1. If 
a trade-off is possible, the platform calculates the optimal thresholds for each protected variable 
to maximise performance while maintaining a level of fairness within the provided constraints. 
Through this system, a model can only be deployed if it is able to produce an acceptable 
performance-balance equilibrium.  
Step 3: Monitoring 
Once a model is deployed to production, there is potential for the model performance to degrade 
over time as the model is retrained on newly labelled data. Similarly, model fairness may also 
potentially degrade over time.  
Every time a model is retrained on new data, model fairness is recomputed and compared with 
the constraints that were previously determined. If the model is no longer able to satisfy these 
constraints, an automated alert is sent to the model developers and approvers. A decision is then 
made to determine whether the model usage should be suspended until a new version is released 
to fix the model fairness degradation.

provides life insurance cover in the event of death and terminal illness. Singlife Account customers 
can also apply for a Singlife Visa debit card with no FX fees and no annual fees.  
The purpose of Singlife Card Transaction Monitoring is to alert the compliance team by identifying 
potentially fraudulent transactions made on Singlife debit cards. Once the alerted transactions are 
validated, and if fraud is confirmed, the compliance team will take further action.  
Model Details 
Identifying fraudulent debit card transactions is an unsupervised business problem which in this use 
case has been considered as an anomaly-detection process where unexpected events in the dataset 
which are different from the normal events are identified. Unexpected events are rare, and their 
characteristics differ significantly from normal data. We have used an autoencoder model to detect 
anomalies in card transactions. An autoencoder is a special type of neural network that copies the 
input values to the output values as shown in Figure 6.10. The number of neurons in the hidden layer 
is less than the input layer, and the hidden layer extracts the essential information from the input 
layer. This condition forces the hidden layer to learn the most significant patterns of the data and 
ignore the “noises”.
Encoding and Decoding  
In the encoding process, input values are compressed to get the hidden layer. The process is a 
mapping from the input space into the lower-dimension latent space, also known as the bottleneck 
layer. The decoding process reconstructs the input layer using the information in the hidden layer. 
Usually the layers between the hidden-input layers and hidden-output layers are symmetrical. There 
is loss in the process, and the model tries to reconstruct the output close to the input. The training 
objective is to minimise reconstruction error. 
Autoencoders are usually used for dimensionality reduction where the number of features in a dataset 
needs to be reduced while preserving the essence of the data. In this experiment, the algorithm has 
been used for anomaly detection, as this is a by-product of the dimensionality reduction. When the 
data is compressed and reconstructed, the main patterns are identified and those outliers that do not 
follow the normal pattern are revealed.

Background  
Standard Chartered selected “Signature Verification” for the Veritas framework use-case

Overview and Problem Statement   
Signature Verification requires the extraction and validation of signatures from cheques as part

incoming signature of a customer with a specimen present in the bank’s systems. This is usually 
performed by a “maker” role and reviewed by a “checker” role.
•	 The comparison is performed visually (i.e., manually) and consequently has the associated 
overheads of any manual process.
•	 Manual judgement is heuristic in nature and often lacks consistency in decision-making. 
•	 The manual process prevents straight-through-processing of transactions and impacts 
the turnaround time for customers.

•	 Language bias: Signatures are primarily written in English and a small percentage are

Alternatively, if the model passes signatures that have been failed by the checker, and 
model performance falls below the threshold of the false acceptance rate, model retraining 
will be triggered since in this case the model is incorrectly passing signatures. 
•	 Historical bias: Traditionally, signature verification is performed against the specimen 
signature provided by the customer at the time of on-boarding. This implies historical data 
around pass or fail is highly dependent on the specimen signature itself, which may be stale.
•	 Measurement bias: During the initial stage of data preparation, signatures are manually 
labelled as pass or fail. Passing/failing a signature is subjective in nature and therefore 
resulted in measurement bias that was identified with the help of the Gage R&R (Repeatability 
and Reproducibility) analysis. Mitigating actions to address this bias have been included in 
the Appendix under Table 3 – Fairness Checklist. Subjectivity in measurement is one of 
the challenges when working with unstructured data.  
•	 Pre-processing bias was also determined to be applicable during noise removal (noise of 
logo, printed text, seals over and above the signature) and colour-scale conversion, thereby 
introducing error into the dataset. This has been addressed at each step by assessing the 
impact of loss in the images. 
Quantification of the above fairness-related biases is a challenge as the use case involves 
unstructured data and is further compounded by the unavailability of Ground Truth related to 
the conditions.  
Several other data-related biases have been assessed: 
There is no impact on the client due to the above data-related biases as there are rectification 
measures in place and additionally there is a human-in-the-loop element where all transactions 
are routed through a checker for manual validation. If a scenario presents where a significant 
volume of signatures is failed by the model due to the above biases, they will be routed to the 
maker queue. This step ensures the overall resilience of the process.  
There are no legal, regulatory or societal considerations based on country-specific requirements in 
this context, and there have been no related points highlighted by the bank’s country compliance 
teams. Fairness and related challenges identified during the initial data collection/analysis phase 
have been socialised with the respective AI system owner.

Specific Achievements and Lessons Learned Through the Journey of AIDA System/AI Model 
Lifecycle 
The recommendations given in the methodology align with current practices at a fundamental level. 
However, there are lessons and opportunities for enhancement:
•	 Standard Chartered has a defined and structured AI Governance lifecycle in place. 
•	 There is value in providing customised training with simplified explanations to the AI system 
owner during the onboarding process to provide guidance on the AI use-case and principles. 
•	 The bank recommends the Veritas methodology is enhanced to include further guidance 
in the use of unstructured data for the reasons given below:
•	 Most of the tools and techniques for assessing algorithmic fairness and system 
explainability that are currently available in the market are designed for structured data. 
In addition, they are not suitable for deep-learning architecture. For this reason, the bank 
uses an in-house tool to verify model fairness in these scenarios. 
•	 The methodology recommends the use of differential privacy, de-identification, data 
anonymisation and data masking to protect personal data. These are challenging to 
apply for unstructured data as masked data cannot be used for training the model.

•	 The methodology does not provide guidance on the safeguards in the use of AI for

2.	 Walk-throughs, deep dives and overview sessions have been held, with these tailored to

6.6.5	 Concluding Remarks      
The bank’s RAI Standards and Principles are based on MAS’s FEAT Principles. The Veritas 
methodology provides strong guidance and recommendations for implementing these principles, 
and at an overall level the bank has implemented a substantial portion of the methodology across 
in-house solutions as well as in onboarded third-party solutions. The methodology has enabled the 
bank to validate the current approach to RAI Governance and identify opportunities to enhance 
the existing practice, which have been detailed in the above sections.  
The bank selected Signature Verification for the use-case integration specifically due to the use 
of unstructured data. The generation and use of unstructured data is growing far faster than 
structured data, and there is an opportunity for financial institutions to improve client services, 
derive insights for business decisions, and solve business problems using AI models that use 
unstructured data.  
The bank’s observation is that the Veritas methodology does not yet fully address implementation 
on AI models with unstructured data, as described in use case study. The bank’s recommendation 
is that the industry continues to collaborate as part of Veritas and provides additional guidance 
specifically to governing the use of deep learning and unstructured data, especially in the context 
of fairness and transparency.

6.7.1	 Overview 
At UOB, we believe in responsible innovation. As we reap the benefits from using Artificial 
Intelligence and Data Analytics (AIDA), it is increasingly important that we are guided by our vision 
and values to do right by our customers and stakeholders. By using AIDA fairly, ethically, with 
accountability and transparency, we strengthen customer trust and create significant business 
value and competitive advantage, achieving the vision of having the most trusted data and insights 
for the world’s most trusted bank. 
With the release of the MAS FEAT Principles, UOB, as one of the lead members of the Veritas 
consortium, collaborated with MAS and its partners to develop the Assessment Methodology 
for Fairness on a credit risk scoring use case and subsequently the Assessment Methodology 
for Ethics and Accountability on a customer marketing use case in Veritas Phase 1 and Phase 2 
respectively.  
The Veritas methodologies have since been applied progressively in UOB to ensure that our 
processes, technology and people align and adhere to the MAS FEAT Principles.  Additionally, 
to establish a broad-based comprehensive implementation approach and to enable a diversity 
of thoughts, a multi-disciplinary Data Ethics Taskforce which comprises senior executive

Managing risk is integral for UOB to create long-term value for our customers and stakeholders. 
Our risk culture demonstrates behaviours that are consistent with our core values – Honourable, 
Enterprising, United and Committed. Being a well-established bank, UOB has frameworks and 
policies in place to manage model risks effectively and comprehensively.  
Integrating the MAS FEAT Principles and Veritas methodologies into the existing governance 
framework has nevertheless presented new challenges. Traditionally, our Model Risk Governance 
Framework focuses on the management, validation and monitoring of risks for compliance and 
regulatory models. With the integration of the MAS FEAT Principles, the scope of assessment has 
been expanded to cover all AIDA models with results that directly impact customers or employees. 
In addition, the Veritas Fairness Toolkit is now used for the assessment of fairness in AIDA models.

Materiality Assessment    
To ensure the risk profiles are commensurate with the materiality levels of the AIDA models, a 
risk-based approach has been adopted to subject only high-materiality models which impact 
customers or employees to a more extensive analysis and validation by the independent Data 
Ethics Validation Team. 
The materiality assessment consists of both quantitative and qualitative considerations to ensure 
that the assessment is comprehensive and holistic. This includes assessing the severity and 
probability of occurrence of harms on the bank from financial, reputational and legal perspectives, 
on customers from a socio-economic perspective, and on regulatory compliance.  
An illustration of the Materiality Assessment Matrix is shown in Figure 6.30. For example, models that 
perform credit scoring are generally assessed to be high materiality as they pose a major risk to the 
bank, and any inherent bias in the models could result in a systematic disadvantage to our customers.

The Journey to Increase RAI Culture and Training    
As the concept of data ethics is relatively new, a broad-based approach has been adopted to 
drive RAI awareness in the organisation across all levels. At the strategic level, an engagement 
session in partnership with an external industry leader was set up to raise awareness to senior 
management on the global AIDA landscape and data ethics principles. At the operational level, 
sharing sessions and workshops were organised to raise awareness on the FEAT Principles. On 
an annual basis, model owners and developers are briefed on the FEAT Principles as part of the 
annual FEAT self-attestation exercise.

6.7.5	 Concluding Remarks   
Since the release of the MAS FEAT Principles, UOB has embarked on our journey to develop and 
implement an effective data ethics governance model by adopting the best practices in data 
ethics. A key learning point in our journey is that data ethics is constantly evolving. Therefore, to 
ensure that our decisions are grounded in our values and guided by our governance framework, it 
is important for us to continuously educate and create awareness on data ethics across all levels 
of the bank. Another key learning point is that a risk-based approach is essential for the bank to 
allocate resources effectively to prioritise and address AIDA risks based on the materiality of the 
models. Moving forward, the roadmap is to enhance the existing integration with best industry 
practices such as the Veritas Toolkit 2.0.  
With the increasing adoption of AI, UOB will continue to support the AI ethics movement and 
advocate for the need to use AIDA responsibly. Through the Veritas projects, we are able to 
contribute to the development of the assessment methodologies for the MAS FEAT Principles. 
We will continue to strengthen this foundation of trust by building upon the best practices in 
Responsible AI and data ethics.

AI and Data Analytics are currently high priorities for FIs. While these technologies offer the promise 
of expanded access to financial products and services as well as improved customer experience, 
they also bring with them new risks on data security and unethical data usage that result in bias 
and discrimination on different grounds. For these reasons, regulatory agencies globally are in the 
process of laying down frameworks and methodologies to address the challenges involved in the 
responsible use of AI within the financial services industry.  
This whitepaper has captured the landscape of Veritas methodology Integration by FIs in Singapore, 
and has illustrated this via the case studies from the seven FIs listed.  
The whitepaper highlights common challenges that FIs faced during integration such as the need 
for additional resources to cover the scope of the work required, as well as the need to refer to 
multiple policy statements across geographies to draft their own governance frameworks.  
In the future, industry will need to address these common challenges to help pave the way for the 
widespread adoption of AI for the benefit of the industry and for society more broadly.
