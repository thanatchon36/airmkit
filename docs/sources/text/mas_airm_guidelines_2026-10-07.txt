Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence Risk Management 
 
1. Introduction  
1.1. The Guidelines on Artificial Intelligence (AI) Risk Management (the “Guidelines”) set out MAS’ 
supervisory expectations relating to AI risk management in financial institutions (FIs)1, and seek to 
support their responsible adoption of AI. These Guidelines set out MAS’ supervisory expectations in the 
areas of oversight, key AI risk management systems, policies, procedures, AI life cycle controls, and 
capabilities and capacity for the use of AI. An overview of the key sections covered in these Guidelines 
is set out below.  
 
Figure 1: Overview of the Guidelines 
1.2. The Guidelines complement existing legislation and guidelines issued by the MAS. They should be read 
in conjunction with relevant guidelines, information papers and circulars that MAS may issue from time 
to time. For instance, existing principles on Fairness, Ethics, Accountability and Transparency (FEAT)2 
continue to apply for guiding the use of AI in the financial sector. FIs should also take into account 
applicable industry standards where appropriate3. The Guidelines apply on a group basis for locally

1.3. For the purpose of these Guidelines, AI refers to machine-based systems or models that derive outputs 
through learned premises such as the data or inputs it receives. The outputs can include estimates, 
predictions, content, summaries, recommendations, or decisions that may influence physical or virtual 
environments. Different AI vary in their levels of autonomy and adaptiveness after deployment 6. 
Calculators or tools whose outputs are solely based on predefined programming logic or rules would 
not be regarded as AI for the purpose of these Guidelines. 
1.4. In addition, the following definitions7 are used:  
(a) A model is a method or approach which converts assumptions and input data into outputs such 
as estimates, decisions, or recommendations.  
(b) A system comprises one or more models and other machine-based components.   
(c) A use case refers to a specific real-world context that the model or system is applied to. 
1.5. The Guidelines establish a set of high-level expectations that all FIs should adhere to as they deploy AI, 
including Generative AI and newer developments such as AI agents. An FI which is part of a global group 
may take guidance from or leverage their Group’s AI risk management frameworks, as long as those 
frameworks meet the expectations set out in these Guidelines.  
1.6. FIs should implement these Guidelines in a manner commensurate with the size and nature of their 
activities, and the extent to which their use of AI could lead to material risks to them. AI can be applied 
to a wide range of use cases, and the risks associated with different use of AI may vary based on the 
scale, scope and business models of FIs.

1.7. Given the pace of AI developments, MAS also expects FIs to regularly review the adequacy of their AI 
risk management efforts against AI developments and address new or accentuated risks that may arise 
due to such developments.   
1.8. These Guidelines take effect on 7 October 2027. FIs may meet the expectations set out in Sections 3 to 
4 from 7 October 2027, and Section 5 and 6 of the Guidelines by 7 October 2028. 
Risks 
1.9. The use of AI can improve performance across business and functional areas but its complexity and 
probabilistic nature can lead to greater uncertainty, as well as unexpected or more biased behaviour 
that is harder to identify compared to the use of simpler methods8. The greater complexity of AI also 
leads to challenges in understanding and explaining its outputs that may be inaccurate or biased. Risks 
from AI use is often an extension of existing risk categories; such risks include: 
(a) Financial risks, e.g., when used for risk management, the greater uncertainty and unexpected 
behaviour of AI could lead to poor risk assessments and consequent financial losses.  
(b) Operational risks, e.g., unexpected behaviour of AI used to automate an FI’s operations could lead 
to operational disruptions or errors in critical processes.  
(c) Conduct risk, e.g., use of AI can result in biased outputs, leading to unfair treatment of certain 
customer groups, a bias for certain investment types over others, or conflicts of interests between 
an FI and its customers.  
(d) Financial crime risks, e.g., when used to support anti-money laundering efforts, the greater 
uncertainty and unexpected behaviour of AI could mean that suspicious transactions go 
undetected.  
(e) Reputational risks, e.g., customer-facing systems, such as chatbots, could provide incorrect 
information or make offensive remarks that lead to negative media attention and reputational 
damage. 
1.10. With Generative AI, such existing risks associated with AI may be amplified. The greater complexity of 
Generative AI gives rise to even greater uncertainty and unexpected behaviour compared to AI. The 
unstructured nature of Generative AI inputs and outputs, and a lack of established techniques in this

area also make it harder to evaluate and test, as well as understand and explain Generative AI’s

coupled with difficulties in evaluating bias of Generative AI outputs, could also result in decisions that 
lead to unfair customer outcomes. Generative AI may also lead to a range of other inter-related risks,

(a) Security risks, e.g., arising from adversarial attacks on Generative AI systems via prompt injection

(b) Privacy risks, e.g., arising from the leakage of confidential or customer data due to the use of third-

(c) Legal and intellectual property risks, e.g., arising from the use of Generative AI trained on data

(d) Third-party AI10 risks, e.g., arising from over-reliance on a few dominant Generative AI providers 
for mission critical areas, or using open-source models with poor security controls.   
(e) Operational risks, e.g., potential service disruptions due to Generative AI outages and problems 
arising from inadequate pre-deployment testing.  
(f) Human-factor risks, e.g., use of shadow AI, poor human oversight over Generative AI used in 
mission critical areas, automation bias or skill degradation due to over-reliance on Generative AI.  
1.11. Similarly, the use of newer technologies such as AI agents, which may be granted greater autonomy 
and access to tools, could further amplify these risks. For example, an AI agent with access to tools 
could autonomously execute unauthorised or erroneous actions that are not aligned with an FI’s 
business objectives or in a customer’s best interests due to a divergence between pre-defined goals 
and how the AI agent translated such goals into actions11. Compromised AI agents with access to 
internal systems and external tools could be used to exfiltrate sensitive data or execute malicious 
commands at scale, amplifying security risks.

2. Application of the Guidelines 
2.1. The Guidelines apply to all FIs in a proportionate manner, according to their size and risk profile.  
2.2. Unless paragraph 2.3. applies, an FI should: 
(a) Establish frameworks, policies and procedures to oversee its use of AI, including clear 
identification of AI use cases, robust risk materiality assessments and an adequate AI inventory, 
as set out in Sections 3 and 4 of these Guidelines. The scope of such oversight, policies and 
procedures may be proportionate to the size and nature of the FI’s activities, its use of AI and its 
risk profile; and 
(b) Apply relevant life cycle controls, and establish adequate capabilities and technology 
infrastructure for the use of AI, as set out in Sections 5 and 6 of these Guidelines. Such controls 
may be calibrated based on their relevance12 to the AI use cases, systems or models in the FI. 
Where such aspects are relevant to the AI use case, system or model, the FI may implement them 
based on the risk materiality of the AI use case.  
2.3. An FI may apply basic AI governance policies and procedures, as set out in paragraph 2.5., if the poor 
performance or unavailability of the AI services or tools used by the FI is unlikely to have a material 
adverse impact on the FI, its customers or other stakeholders. In assessing the potential impact, the FI 
should consider: 
(a)  the potential financial, operational, regulatory, legal or reputational impact on the FI; and 
(b) the potential impact on customers or other stakeholders, including in relation to fairness, ethical 
conduct and consumer protection. 
2.4. Examples of AI use13 that would generally be regarded as meeting the criteria in paragraph 2.3. are: 
(a) Using AI to assist in drafting, proofreading or rephrasing emails to customers; 
(b) Using AI to assist in summarising documents or meeting notes for internal reference;

(d) Using AI to assist in generating formulas, charts or visualisations for internal reference; 
(e) Using AI image generators to assist in designing marketing or internal materials; and 
(f) Using AI-powered chatbots to assist personnel in locating relevant internal resources, such as

2.5. Basic AI governance policies and procedures should minimally include the following: 
(a) clear accountability for AI oversight (e.g., by designating a member of senior management as

3. AI Oversight 
3.1. The board of directors and senior management of an FI play critical roles in establishing and overseeing 
robust frameworks, policies and procedures to support AI risk management across the FI.   
3.2. The board and senior management should ensure that existing risk management frameworks, 
policies, and practices across the organisation14 adequately identify, assess, and address risks posed 
by AI. Board and senior management should maintain effective oversight of risks from AI use, foster 
the appropriate risk culture for the use of AI, and ensure that the FI’s use of AI would not conflict with 
its ability to meet regulatory requirements or other supervisory expectations15.  
3.3. The board and senior management should ensure consistent standards, clear accountability, and 
robust coordination across the FI to manage risks from AI use. While the FI may adopt varying 
approaches to AI risk management, such as creating a new centralised function to manage all risks from 
AI use, or managing the incremental risks from AI use via existing risk management functions, the board 
and senior management should still ensure consistent and coordinated management of risks from AI 
use across all relevant functions16.  
3.4. The board, or a committee17 delegated by it, is responsible for:   
(a) approving and regularly reviewing the overall governance approach for AI risk management, 
including the strategic direction for AI use and key frameworks, structures, policies and 
procedures designed to assess and manage risks from the FI’s use of AI on an ongoing basis;  
(b) ensuring that risks from AI use are explicitly addressed within the FI’s risk appetite framework, 
including qualitative statements and quantitative measures for such risks as appropriate18;

4. Key AI Risk Management Systems, Policies 
and Procedures 
4.1. An FI should ensure that its AI risk management framework encompasses key systems, policies and 
procedures for the identification, inventorisation, and risk materiality assessment of AI. The FI should 
apply consistent and robust approaches to identify, inventorise and determine the risk materiality of 
AI, and apply controls proportionate to the assessed risk materiality.   
AI Identification 
4.2. An FI should establish systems, policies and procedures to ensure the consistent identification of AI 
use across all relevant business and functional areas. This identification process is a critical 
prerequisite for applying the appropriate governance, risk management standards, and controls 
effectively to AI throughout their lifecycle. Clear AI definitions, criteria and processes, supported by 
tools and systems as appropriate, should be implemented to facilitate this identification process. 
Where AI is incorporated within21, or used in the delivery of, third-party services, the identification 
process should minimally cover such AI in services provided by material third-party service providers.  
4.3. An FI should assign clear roles and responsibilities for AI identification, including the designation of 
a control function to be responsible for AI identification systems and processes. While the actual 
identification of AI use may be carried out by relevant business or functional units, the designated 
control function should retain sufficient and independent oversight to ensure the consistent application 
of the identification process across the FI including the identification of new AI use over time. It should 
also be the final arbiter on whether a given use constitutes AI use. The designated control function 
should ensure that clear documentation of the identification process and outcomes is maintained, and 
that identification systems and processes are regularly reviewed and updated to take into account 
newer AI technologies or introduction of AI into existing third-party services. 
4.4. An FI should ensure that residual risks from unidentified AI use remain within the FI's risk appetite. 
Where there are practical constraints in consistently identifying all AI use, e.g., due to inadequate

AI Inventory 
4.5. An FI should establish and maintain an accurate inventory of AI use cases, systems or models across 
the FI to the extent possible or practicable, so as to support governance and oversight, as well as risk 
management, throughout the AI lifecycle. There should be clear policies and procedures on the 
maintenance of the inventory, including establishing an appropriate frequency to update the inventory 
to reflect new, updated or decommissioned AI use cases, systems or models.  
4.6. The FI can enhance existing inventories to include AI use cases, systems or models or establish a 
dedicated AI inventory. In either case, there should be clear linkages between the AI inventory and 
other relevant inventories23 in the FI, to facilitate cross-referencing between inventories as part of 
effective oversight.   
4.7. The AI inventory should capture key attributes at an appropriate level of granularity to enable 
effective governance and oversight, as well as risk management. Specific attributes may vary based 
on the FI’s context or the nature of the AI use case, system, or model24, but they may include the 
following: purpose and description, approved scope of use (e.g., jurisdiction), model type, data used, 
dependencies, lifecycle status, assigned risk materiality rating, model review status, key roles and 
responsibilities (e.g., owners, developers), and links to essential documentation. Maintaining this 
information facilitates risk assessment, monitoring of scope adherence, and consistent application of 
controls.

into account newer AI technologies where there may be additional relevant attributes25 or guardrails 
needed, or additional information relating to third-party AI. 
4.9. The FI should assign clear roles and responsibilities for the inventorisation of AI, including the

out by relevant business or functional units, the designated control function should retain sufficient 
and independent oversight to ensure that the AI inventory remains accurate and complete.   
AI Risk Materiality Assessment 
4.10. An FI should establish an assessment methodology to evaluate the risk materiality of an AI use case 
based on the nature of its business and the risks arising from the systems and models used26. The 
assessment methodology should be applied consistently to perform risk materiality assessments for 
each AI use case in the FI. Such assessments are critical for calibrating the risk management approach, 
ensuring that controls are applied proportionately to the risks. This ensures that AI use cases assessed 
as having higher risk materiality receive more stringent scrutiny and robust controls, supporting 
effective oversight and confirming that its use remains within approved boundaries.   
4.11. The assessment should take into account both the inherent risk materiality of an AI use case before 
the appropriate risk management controls are applied, as well as the residual risk materiality after 
risk management controls are applied. The FI should ensure that the residual risk materiality of the AI 
use case meets the FI’s risk appetite before deployment. The risk materiality assessment methodology 
and risk materiality assessments for each AI use case should also be regularly reviewed to ensure their 
continued relevance and appropriateness.   
4.12. The risk materiality assessment of an AI use case should consider various risk dimensions relevant to 
the FI’s context, minimally covering:  
(a) Impact: The potential consequences of a failure, malfunction, or poor performance of the AI on 
the FI (e.g., financial, operational, regulatory, reputational) and its customers or other

be implemented by relevant business or functional units, the designated control function should retain 
sufficient and independent oversight to ensure the consistent application of the assessment process 
across the FI. This includes being the arbitrator or approver of risk materiality assessments, and 
ensuring that such assessments are clearly documented and regularly reviewed for accuracy and 
relevance.

5. AI Life Cycle27 Controls 
5.1. An FI should plan for and implement robust controls covering the entire life cycle of an AI use case, 
system or model and assign clear roles and responsibilities for such controls across different business 
and functional units. AI life cycle controls should be regularly reviewed to take into account the use of 
newer AI technologies.   
5.2. An FI may implement the relevant AI lifecycle controls in a proportionate manner, ensuring that they 
are appropriate to the assessed risk materiality of the AI use case28. Where there are practical 
constraints, e.g., where there may not be adequate disclosure of information by the third-party AI 
provider, the FI should identify risks that arise from such constraints, and put in place the necessary 
mitigants, such as limiting the use of AI. After putting in place such mitigants, the FI should ensure that 
residual risks remain within the risk appetite of the FI. 
5.3. For an AI use case assessed as high risk, an FI should develop and implement contingency plans 
outlining fallback options, such as alternative systems or manual processes, to ensure business 
continuity in case of AI failure or unexpected behaviour. Plans should be regularly reviewed and tested 
to ensure that they are operationally feasible under a range of operating conditions. For AI with "kill 
switches"29, clear contingency activation protocols should be in place and tested regularly.   
Data Management 
5.4. An FI should put in place data management controls to ensure data used across the AI life cycle is fit 
for purpose and representative, of high quality, and subject to robust data governance. General data 
governance and management standards, in areas such as data ownership, access controls, and 
intellectual property rights, should be applied to data used for AI, and, where necessary, uplifted to 
address AI-specific requirements. The FI should ensure existing data governance controls are adequate

(d) Data Classification: Appropriate data classification processes to guide appropriate use of data in

Transparency and Explainability 
5.5. An FI should establish relevant controls 31 to ensure an appropriate level of transparency and 
explainability32 for an AI use case. Transparency is important to support accountability and trust in the 
use of AI, and enables customers, internal users and other stakeholders to make informed assessments 
of the risks, reliability and limitations of an AI use case, system or model. It also allows an FI to 
demonstrate that their use of AI is in line with their stated objectives and risk management standards, 
and helps to strengthen trust among customers and other stakeholders.  
5.6. The degree of transparency and explainability required should reflect the context and nature of the 
AI use case33 and its assessed risk materiality. For example, AI that is heavily relied upon for credit 
decisioning, insurance underwriting or other regulated activities with high impact on customer 
outcomes (e.g., provision of financial advisory services or fund management), will require a higher level 
of transparency and explainability than AI used solely to assist internal users in low-risk tasks. For AI 
use cases requiring higher levels of transparency and explainability, the FI should pay greater attention 
to the different features or attributes in data used as inputs and justifications for their use, the ability 
for users to identify key drivers of the output, the need to inform customers of the use of AI, the 
consequences of AI-driven decisions, and channels for redress. The needs of the intended audience 
should also be considered when determining an appropriate manner to provide transparency and 
explainability for the AI use case34.

Fairness 
5.7. An FI should define what it considers “fair” outcomes35 and have appropriate controls to identify and 
mitigate harmful biases and discriminatory outcomes across the AI life cycle. Controls should be 
calibrated to the context and nature of the AI use case and its assessed risk materiality. For example, 
greater attention should be paid to AI used in areas such as credit decisioning or insurance underwriting 
that may lead to unfair access or denial of financial services or products offered to individuals.   
5.8. Where fairness considerations are relevant, the FI should conduct fairness assessments, which may 
involve defining relevant protected attributes36, assessing whether the use of AI leads to systematic 
disadvantages for specific groups using appropriate fairness metrics, and documenting the results and 
any mitigation steps taken.   
Human Oversight 
5.9. An FI should put in place and regularly review controls to ensure appropriate human oversight over 
an AI use case, system or model37 across its life cycle. The need for and degree of human oversight 
should take into account the objective of using AI, and be proportionate to the risk materiality of the 
AI use case. An FI should also take into account automation bias and decision fatigue as the use of AI 
increases in speed and scale. Key areas that the FI should consider include: 
(a) Roles and Responsibilities: Clear assignment of roles and responsibilities for human oversight, 
including escalation and decision-making processes relating to human oversight.  
(b) Capabilities: Equipping competent personnel assigned to monitor AI use with the necessary 
capabilities, including the necessary authority and ability to intervene.  
(c) Design: Designing and developing AI systems or models from the outset to enable and facilitate 
appropriate human oversight. These include mechanisms to escalate AI outputs or 
recommendations for human review where pre-defined conditions (e.g., on reliability and 
accuracy) are met.

Post-Deployment Monitoring and Review 
5.23. An FI should develop and implement comprehensive and robust controls for the ongoing monitoring 
of all deployed AI (including third-party AI used in the FI). Given the uncertainties associated with AI, 
their dynamic nature and the potential for model staleness and performance degradation due to data 
or model drifts over time, ongoing monitoring is critical to ensure that deployed AI operates as intended 
and remains fit for purpose over time. The frequency and intensity of monitoring activities should be 
proportionate to the assessed risk materiality of the deployed AI. Key areas that the FI should consider 
include: 
(a) Monitoring Measures: Based on the risks of the AI use case, define key metrics to be monitored 
and acceptable performance thresholds for each metric. Metrics may include relevant dimensions 
like robustness, stability, data quality, and fairness. Tiered thresholds (e.g., early warning 
threshold levels) should be considered to pre-empt deterioration and trigger early actions. 
Appropriate checks for data drifts (changes in input data distributions), concept drifts (changes in 
relationships between inputs and outputs), and overall model drifts should be implemented.

as reasoning processes, actions taken, tools used should also be monitored.

6. AI Capability & Capacity 
AI Risk Management Capabilities 
6.1. An FI should determine and ensure the necessary competence and proper conduct of personnel 
involved in developing, deploying and using AI. This includes recruiting the necessary talent and 
providing appropriate training and support to equip employees with the skills, knowledge, and culture 
for the appropriate use of AI, as well as effective AI risk management. Adequate resources, including 
human, technological, and financial, should be allocated proportionate to the risk profile of the AI use 
case and their underlying systems or models.   
6.2. Regular reviews should be conducted to ensure that the relevant personnel involved in developing, 
deploying and maintaining an AI use case, system or model are equipped with adequate capabilities 
and capacity for effective AI risk management. This includes regularly reviewing programmes for 
building capabilities and capacity for effective AI risk management to ensure that they are up to date 
and incorporate training to address risks associated with newer AI technologies. 
Technology Infrastructure for AI 
6.3. An FI should ensure that its technology infrastructure is adequate for the AI use cases, systems or 
models it wants to implement. Like other information technology systems, AI systems and models used 
in such systems are also subject to technology risks, including system availability, resilience, safety and 
cybersecurity risks. To address these risks, the FI should ensure that the underlying hardware and 
software resources, such as graphics processing units, network infrastructure, system memory, secure 
data pipelines are sufficient to meet performance, scalability, and resilience needs for the AI use case, 
system or model. In determining the appropriate technology infrastructure for a specific AI use case, 
system or model, the FI should take into account relevant technology risk management guidelines and 
notices50, as well as relevant industry frameworks51.
